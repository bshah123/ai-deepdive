By the end of this lesson you will be able to write a supervised learning problem as a data distribution, a hypothesis class and a loss; compute the empirical risk of a model by hand and in NumPy; and explain why the model with the lowest training error can still be the wrong model.

**You need:** Python functions (Lesson 7.3), NumPy arrays (Lesson 15.1), and the mean and square of a list of numbers.

## The problem learning solves

Suppose you have records of past houses (floor area and sale price) and want to estimate the price of a house you have not seen. No formula is given. Learning means choosing a rule from examples that also works on houses you have not seen. Two things make this hard. You only observe the examples you have, and the rule that fits those examples best is not guaranteed to work on new ones.

## Formulating the problem

A supervised learning problem has five ingredients.

- **Inputs** $x$ from a space $\mathcal{X}$ (floor area) and **outputs** $y$ from a space $\mathcal{Y}$ (price).
- An unknown **data distribution** $D$ over pairs $(x, y)$. We assume each pair is drawn independently from $D$ and all pairs share that distribution (the i.i.d. assumption).
- A **hypothesis class** $\mathcal{H}$: the set of candidate rules $h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ the learner may choose from, for example all straight lines through the origin, $h_w(x) = w x$.
- A **loss** $\ell(h(x), y) \ge 0$ that scores one prediction. Squared error $(h(x) - y)^2$ is common for regression. For classification, the 0-1 loss is 1 when the prediction is wrong and 0 when it is right.
- The **true risk** $R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim D}\,[\ell(h(x), y)]$, the average loss over all of $D$. Here $\mathbb{E}$ is the expectation, the average over the distribution.

Supervised means every example carries a label $y$. Unsupervised learning has only $x$ and a different goal, such as clustering or density estimation, so the risk formulation in this lesson does not cover it.

The catch is that $D$ is unknown, so $R(h)$ cannot be computed. We only have a sample.

## Empirical risk

Given a sample $S$ of $n$ pairs $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$, the **empirical risk** is the average loss over the sample:

$$R_S(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \ell\big(h(x_i), y_i\big)$$

**Empirical risk minimisation (ERM)** returns the hypothesis with the smallest empirical risk: $h_S = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} R_S(h)$. Least squares regression is ERM with squared loss over linear hypotheses. The hope is that $R_S(h_S)$ is a good estimate of $R(h_S)$. Whether it is depends on the sample size, the size of $\mathcal{H}$ and the loss. Lessons 19.4 and 19.5 make this precise.

## Worked example, by hand

Take four points $(x, y) = (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5), (4, 4)$, the class $h_w(x) = wx$ and squared loss. The empirical risk is

$$R_S(w) = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4} (w x_i - y_i)^2$$

At $w = 1$, the predictions are $1, 2, 3, 4$. The residuals (prediction minus target) are $-1, -1, -2, 0$, their squares are $1, 1, 4, 0$, and the sum is 6. So $R_S(1) = 6/4 = 1.5$.

At $w = 1.5$, the predictions are $1.5, 3, 4.5, 6$. The residuals are $-0.5, 0, -0.5, 2$, the squares sum to 4.5, and $R_S(1.5) = 1.125$. A different slope gives a lower risk, so we ask which slope is best.

Expand the square and sum over the four points. We need $\sum x_i^2 = 30$, $\sum x_i y_i = 39$ and $\sum y_i^2 = 54$:

$$R_S(w) = \frac{1}{4}\left(30 w^2 - 78 w + 54\right) = 7.5\,w^2 - 19.5\,w + 13.5$$

This parabola opens upwards, so its minimum is where the derivative is zero: $15 w - 19.5 = 0$, which gives $w^* = 1.3$. This equals $\sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2 = 39/30$. Substituting back, $R_S(1.3) = 0.825$.

![Four points with the best-fit line h(x) = 1.3x and dashed residual segments, with the squared residuals 0.49, 0.16, 1.21 and 1.44 summing to 3.30](diagrams/19.1-worked-example.svg "The best slope for four points: the residuals at w* = 1.3.")

![The empirical risk R_S(w) on the four points as a parabola in w, with the marked values at w = 1, 1.5 and the minimum at w* = 1.3](diagrams/19.1-risk-curve.svg "Empirical risk as a function of the slope: the minimum is at 1.3.")

The code below uses NumPy only and reproduces these numbers.

```python
import numpy as np

# Four training examples (x_i, y_i); hypothesis h_w(x) = w * x; squared loss
x = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0])
y = np.array([2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 4.0])

def empirical_risk(w):
    """Average squared loss of h_w on the training set."""
    return np.mean((w * x - y) ** 2)

print("R_S(1.0) =", empirical_risk(1.0))
print("R_S(1.5) =", empirical_risk(1.5))

# Closed form: setting dR_S/dw = 0 gives w* = sum(x*y) / sum(x*x)
w_star = np.sum(x * y) / np.sum(x * x)
print("w* =", w_star, " R_S(w*) =", round(empirical_risk(w_star), 4))

# Brute-force check on a grid
grid = np.linspace(0, 3, 30001)
print("grid minimiser =", round(grid[np.argmin([empirical_risk(w) for w in grid])], 4))
```

```output
R_S(1.0) = 1.5
R_S(1.5) = 1.125
w* = 1.3  R_S(w*) = 0.825
grid minimiser = 1.3
```

## Overfitting: low training risk is not enough

The worked example fits and scores on the same four points. A real model must be scored on data it did not see. To see how this goes wrong, we run a controlled experiment. The true rule is $y = \sin(\pi x) + \varepsilon$, with $x$ uniform on $[-1, 1]$ and $\varepsilon$ Gaussian with standard deviation 0.3, so its variance is 0.09. Each run draws 20 training points, fits polynomials of degree 1 to 12 by least squares (ERM over each polynomial class), and estimates the true risk on 20,000 fresh points. The whole experiment is repeated for 200 independent training sets.

Two facts frame the numbers. Both are reasoning from first principles, not results from a paper.

- Degree-$d$ polynomials are a subset of degree-$(d+1)$ polynomials (set the new top coefficient to zero). On a fixed training set, the minimum over the larger class cannot be larger, so the training risk never rises with the degree. We checked this in all 200 runs.
- The lowest squared-error risk any predictor can reach here is the noise variance, 0.09, achieved by predicting $\sin(\pi x)$ exactly.

![Log-scale chart of mean training risk and mean true risk against polynomial degree 1 to 12: training risk falls steadily, true risk is lowest at degree 3 and rises steeply from degree 6, with a dashed line at the noise floor 0.09](diagrams/19.1-sweep.svg "Training risk keeps falling, true risk is U-shaped.")

```python
import numpy as np

NOISE, N_TRAIN, N_TEST, REPS = 0.3, 20, 20000, 200
DEGREES = range(1, 13)

def one_sweep(seed):
    """Fit polynomials of each degree to one training set; return train and test MSE."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x_tr = rng.uniform(-1, 1, N_TRAIN)
    y_tr = np.sin(np.pi * x_tr) + rng.normal(0, NOISE, N_TRAIN)
    x_te = rng.uniform(-1, 1, N_TEST)
    y_te = np.sin(np.pi * x_te) + rng.normal(0, NOISE, N_TEST)
    train, test = [], []
    for d in DEGREES:
        coef = np.polyfit(x_tr, y_tr, d)   # ERM: least squares over degree-d polynomials
        train.append(np.mean((np.polyval(coef, x_tr) - y_tr) ** 2))
        test.append(np.mean((np.polyval(coef, x_te) - y_te) ** 2))
    return train, test

runs = [one_sweep(seed) for seed in range(REPS)]
train = np.array([r[0] for r in runs])
test = np.array([r[1] for r in runs])

print("degree  train(mean)  true(mean)  true(median)")
for i, d in enumerate(DEGREES):
    print(f"{d:6d}  {train[:, i].mean():11.4f}  {test[:, i].mean():10.4f}  {np.median(test[:, i]):12.4f}")
```

Read the table in three regions.

- **Degree 1 underfits.** A straight line cannot follow a sine curve, so both the training risk (0.248) and the true risk (0.320) are high.
- **Degree 3 is the best choice.** Its mean true risk, 0.118, is the lowest. It sits above the floor of 0.09 because 20 points leave some estimation error.
- **Beyond degree 3, the model overfits.** The training risk keeps falling, to 0.029 at degree 12, while the true risk rises. The model is fitting the noise in its 20 training points.

The degree-12 mean is 8.0 × 10^7, but its median is 59.2. A few training sets produce fits that blow up near $x = \pm 1$, and the mean is dominated by them. The degree-12 design matrix is ill-conditioned (condition number up to 4.5 × 10^7 across the 200 runs), so its last digits depend on floating-point arithmetic. We also ran the same experiment in a Legendre basis, which spans the same polynomials. Its mean agrees to about one part in 10^9, so the blow-up is not an artefact of the monomial basis. The output above is from NumPy 2.5.5 on CPython 3.12.13.

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Training risk is optimistic.** $R_S(h_S)$ is the minimum you chose, so it is biased downwards. Report it as training risk only. Score the final model once on a held-out test set that was not used for any choice.

> [!TRAP]
> **The loss is a choice.** Minimising squared error does not minimise the number of classification mistakes. A model can have a lower squared-error risk and still make more wrong predictions than another.

> [!TRAP]
> **A mean can hide a spread.** At degree 12 the mean true risk is 8.0 × 10^7 and the median is 59.2. When a few runs dominate, report the median or the spread over runs as well.

> [!TRAP]
> **$R_S$ approximates $R$ only for data drawn from $D$.** If the test data come from a different distribution, the held-out number measures a different risk, and the sample-based estimate does not apply.

## Summary

- A supervised problem is specified by a data distribution $D$, a hypothesis class $\mathcal{H}$ and a loss $\ell$. The true risk $R(h)$ averages the loss over $D$, which is unknown.
- The empirical risk $R_S(h)$ averages the loss over a sample. Empirical risk minimisation (ERM) returns $h_S = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} R_S(h)$.
- On four points with $h_w(x) = wx$, $R_S(w) = 7.5w^2 - 19.5w + 13.5$, with minimiser $w^* = 1.3$ and $R_S(w^*) = 0.825$.
- Training risk falls as the model grows, but true risk is U-shaped. Degree 3 has the lowest true risk (0.118) and degree 12 the highest. Low training risk is not evidence of a good model.

## Quiz time

### Question 1: Calculation

Using the worked-example data ($x = 1, 2, 3, 4$; $y = 2, 3, 5, 4$) and $h_w(x) = wx$ with squared loss, what is $R_S(2)$?

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

The predictions are $2, 4, 6, 8$. The residuals are $0, 1, 1, 4$, and their squares sum to $0 + 1 + 1 + 16 = 18$. So $R_S(2) = 18/4 = 4.5$.

Check with the closed form at $w = 2$: $7.5 \cdot 4 - 19.5 \cdot 2 + 13.5 = 30 - 39 + 13.5 = 4.5$.

</details>

### Question 2: Explanation

On a fixed training set, why can the training risk of the best degree-$d$ polynomial never increase when $d$ goes up by one?

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

The degree-$d$ polynomials are a subset of the degree-$(d+1)$ polynomials, because setting the new top coefficient to zero gives a degree-$d$ polynomial. The minimum of the training risk over a larger set cannot exceed the minimum over a subset. Equality is possible, and the bound says nothing about the true risk, which can rise even as the training risk falls.

</details>

### Question 3: Bug-spotting

A student writes this function and then compares its values for a 1,000-point dataset and a 4-point dataset:

```python
def empirical_risk(w, x, y):
    return np.sum((w * x - y) ** 2)
```

What is wrong, and what is the fix?

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

The function sums the losses instead of averaging them, so its value grows with $n$. For a fixed dataset, the minimiser over $w$ is unchanged, because multiplying by $1/n$ does not move the argmin. But the values from datasets of different sizes are not comparable. The fix is `np.mean((w * x - y) ** 2)`, which divides by $n$.

</details>

### Question 4: Diagnosis

A model has training risk 0.02 and held-out risk 0.50. Name two causes you would check and one check for each.

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

1. **Overfitting.** The model class is too flexible for the sample size. Check the held-out risk across model sizes: if it is U-shaped, as in the degree sweep above, the model is too large.
2. **Distribution shift or leakage.** The held-out data may come from a different process than the training data, or preprocessing may have used held-out data. Check where each split came from, and fit preprocessing on the training split only.

Both causes give the same symptom, so the check that separates them is the source of the split, not the loss curve.

</details>
