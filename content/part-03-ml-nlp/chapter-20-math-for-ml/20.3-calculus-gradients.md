This lesson gives you the three derivative objects that gradient-based training uses: the **gradient** of a scalar loss, the **Jacobian** of a vector-valued map, and the **Hessian** of second derivatives. You will compute each by hand, check it with finite differences in NumPy, and use a second-order Taylor expansion to predict how a loss changes after a step.

**You need:** vectors, dot products and matrices (Lesson 20.1), and single-variable derivatives.

## The problem this solves

A neural network loss depends on thousands to billions of weights, collected in a vector $w$. Training asks two questions. If I change $w$ by a small step $d$, how much does the loss change? Which $d$ decreases it fastest? A single derivative cannot answer either, because a step can point in any direction. We need the slope in every direction at once, and a way to predict the change from it.

## Gradient: the slope in every direction

For a scalar function $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$, the **partial derivative** $\partial f / \partial x_i$ differentiates with respect to $x_i$ and holds the other variables fixed. The **gradient** collects them into a vector:

$$
\nabla f(x) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right)
$$

Take $f(w) = w_0^2 + w_1^2$ at $w = (1, 2)$. Holding $w_1$ fixed, $\partial f / \partial w_0 = 2 w_0 = 2$. Holding $w_0$ fixed, $\partial f / \partial w_1 = 2 w_1 = 4$. So $\nabla f = (2, 4)$.

![Level curves of f = w0^2 + w1^2 as circles, with the gradient (2, 4) at the point (1, 2) pointing outward, perpendicular to the circle through that point](diagrams/20.3-gradient-contour.svg "Level curves (contours) of f. The gradient is perpendicular to the contour through the point and points uphill.")

The gradient matters because of the first-order Taylor expansion, derived later in this lesson: $f(x + d) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x) \cdot d$. The change is largest when $d$ points along $\nabla f$, and zero when $d$ is tangent to a level curve. Gradient descent therefore steps along $-\nabla f$.

## Jacobian: when the output is a vector

Now let $F: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ return $m$ numbers, as one layer of a network does. Its **Jacobian** $J \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ has entries $J_{ij} = \partial F_i / \partial x_j$. Row $i$ is the gradient of output $i$. Column $j$ shows how every output responds to input $j$.

Worked example: $F(x) = (x_0^2 + x_1^2,\ x_0 x_1)$. Then

$$
J(x) = \begin{pmatrix} 2x_0 & 2x_1 \\ x_1 & x_0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(1, 2) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad F(1, 2) = (5, 2).
$$

![A two-input map F, its value F(1, 2) = (5, 2), and the 2 by 2 Jacobian matrix of partial derivatives at (1, 2)](diagrams/20.3-jacobian-shape.svg "Rows are outputs, columns are inputs. The shape of J is always outputs by inputs.")

The Jacobian's main use is the **chain rule**. If $g = h \circ F$ with a scalar $h: \mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}$, then

$$
\nabla g(x) = J_F(x)^\top \, \nabla h(F(x)).
$$

Derivation: $\partial g / \partial x_j = \sum_i (\partial h / \partial y_i)(\partial F_i / \partial x_j)$, which is entry $j$ of $J^\top \nabla h$. Check it with $h(y) = y_0 + 2 y_1$, so $\nabla h = (1, 2)$. Then $J^\top (1, 2)^\top = (2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2,\ 4 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2) = (6, 6)$. Directly, $g(x) = h(F(x)) = (x_0 + x_1)^2$, so $\nabla g = 2(x_0 + x_1)(1, 1) = (6, 6)$ at $(1, 2)$. Backpropagation applies this rule layer by layer, multiplying transposed Jacobians in reverse order (my reasoning, not from a cited source).

## Hessian: curvature

The **Hessian** of a scalar $f$ is the matrix of second partials, $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$. If the second partials are continuous, $H$ is symmetric (Schwarz's theorem, a standard result I have not checked against a source here). Its eigenvalues give the curvature along the eigenvector directions. At a critical point, where $\nabla f = 0$ (the standard second-derivative test; I have not checked it against a source here):

- all eigenvalues positive: local minimum;
- all eigenvalues negative: local maximum;
- mixed signs: saddle point.

A zero eigenvalue leaves the test inconclusive along that direction.

![Three quadratics, their Hessians and eigenvalues: x^2 + y^2 is a bowl, x^2 - y^2 is a saddle, and -x^2 - y^2 is a dome](diagrams/20.3-hessian-signs.svg "Eigenvalue signs classify a critical point. The three Hessians here are diagonal, so their eigenvalues are the diagonal entries.")

Saddle points are not rare in high dimensions. Dauphin et al. argue that the main difficulty in minimising high-dimensional non-convex error functions comes from the proliferation of saddle points, not local minima [1].

A realistic Hessian: the one-sample logistic loss $\ell(w) = \log(1 + e^{a \cdot w})$ with $a = (1, 2)$. Write $z = a \cdot w$ and $\sigma(z) = 1 / (1 + e^{-z})$. Since $\frac{d}{dz} \log(1 + e^z) = \sigma(z)$ and $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$,

$$
\nabla \ell(w) = \sigma(z)\, a, \qquad H(w) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)\, a a^\top.
$$

At $w = 0$, $z = 0$ and $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, so $\nabla \ell = (0.5, 1)$ and $H = 0.25 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.25 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.

The NumPy block below checks the gradient, the Jacobian and this Hessian with central differences, which you will meet properly in the last section. The Hessian's eigenvalues are $0$ and $1.25$. The matrix is rank one because $\ell$ depends on $w$ only through $z$. The zero eigenvalue points along $(2, -1)$, which is orthogonal to $a$: the loss is convex but flat in that direction.

```python
import numpy as np

np.set_printoptions(precision=4, suppress=True)

def central_grad(f, x, h=1e-5):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    g = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(x.size):
        e = np.zeros_like(x)
        e[i] = h
        g[i] = (f(x + e) - f(x - e)) / (2 * h)          # central difference: error is O(h^2)
    return g

def central_jac(F, x, h=1e-5):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    J = np.zeros((np.size(F(x)), x.size))               # rows = outputs, columns = inputs
    for i in range(x.size):
        e = np.zeros_like(x)
        e[i] = h
        J[:, i] = (F(x + e) - F(x - e)) / (2 * h)
    return J

def central_hess(f, x, h=1e-4):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    n = x.size
    H = np.zeros((n, n))
    E = np.eye(n) * h
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            if i == j:
                H[i, i] = (f(x + E[i]) - 2 * f(x) + f(x - E[i])) / h**2
            else:
                H[i, j] = (f(x + E[i] + E[j]) - f(x + E[i] - E[j])
                           - f(x - E[i] + E[j]) + f(x - E[i] - E[j])) / (4 * h * h)
    return H

# Example 1: gradient of q(w) = w0^2 + w1^2 at (1, 2). Analytic: (2*w0, 2*w1) = (2, 4).
q = lambda w: w[0]**2 + w[1]**2
print("gradient of q at (1, 2):", central_grad(q, [1.0, 2.0]))

# Example 2: F(x) = (x0^2 + x1^2, x0*x1). Analytic Jacobian [[2*x0, 2*x1], [x1, x0]] = [[2, 4], [2, 1]].
F = lambda x: np.array([x[0]**2 + x[1]**2, x[0] * x[1]])
print("Jacobian of F at (1, 2):")
print(central_jac(F, [1.0, 2.0]))

# Example 3: one-sample logistic loss l(w) = log(1 + exp(a.w)), a = (1, 2), at w = 0.
# Analytic: gradient sigma(z) a and Hessian sigma(z)(1 - sigma(z)) a a^T, with z = a.w = 0.
a = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
loss = lambda w: np.logaddexp(0.0, a @ w)            # logaddexp avoids overflow in log(1 + exp(z))
w0 = np.zeros(2)
print("gradient of loss at w = 0:", central_grad(loss, w0))
H = central_hess(loss, w0)
print("Hessian of loss at w = 0:")
print(H)
H_exact = 0.25 * np.outer(a, a)                       # sigma(0) = 0.5, so sigma(1 - sigma) = 0.25
print("max |numeric - analytic| Hessian:", f"{np.abs(H - H_exact).max():.1e}")
print("eigenvalues of Hessian:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(H_exact))

# Chain rule: g(x) = h(F(x)) with h(y) = y0 + 2*y1, so grad g = J^T grad h.
hy = lambda y: y[0] + 2 * y[1]
print("numeric grad of h(F(x)) at (1, 2):", central_grad(lambda x: hy(F(x)), [1.0, 2.0]))
J = central_jac(F, [1.0, 2.0])
print("J^T grad h:", J.T @ np.array([1.0, 2.0]))
```

## Taylor series: predicting a step

For a smooth $f$, the second-order Taylor expansion at $x$ for a step $d$ is

$$
f(x + d) = f(x) + \nabla f(x) \cdot d + \tfrac{1}{2}\, d^\top H(x)\, d + R,
$$

where, if the third derivatives are bounded near $x$, the remainder satisfies $|R| \le C \|d\|^3$ for some constant $C$ (Taylor's theorem with remainder; my reasoning). The first two terms form a linear model. Adding the quadratic term also captures curvature. The approximation is local: its error grows like $\|d\|^3$ in general.

Take the loss along the line $w = t\,(1, 0)$, starting from $w = 0$. Then $\phi(t) = \log(1 + e^t)$, with $\phi(0) = \log 2 \approx 0.693147$, $\phi'(0) = \sigma(0) = 0.5$ and $\phi''(0) = 0.25$. The two models are

$$
T_1(t) = \log 2 + 0.5\,t, \qquad T_2(t) = \log 2 + 0.5\,t + 0.125\,t^2.
$$

At $t = 1$ the true value is $1.313262$, $T_1 = 1.193147$, $T_2 = 1.318147$, so the second-order error is $-0.004885$. At $t = 2$ the true value is $2.126928$, $T_1 = 1.693147$, $T_2 = 2.193147$, and the error is $-0.066219$.

![The true loss along one line, with first-order (tangent) and second-order Taylor models; the second-order curve follows the true curve much further out](diagrams/20.3-logistic-taylor.svg "Taylor models of the loss along the line w = t (1, 0). Blue dots mark t = 1 and t = 2.")

The cubic term happens to vanish here, because $\phi''' = \sigma(1 - \sigma)(1 - 2\sigma)$ is zero at $\sigma = 0.5$. The error at these points is therefore set by the fourth-order term. The $\|d\|^3$ bound is the general guarantee, not this example's rate.

Taylor models also give a training rule. Minimising the quadratic model $\nabla f \cdot d + \tfrac{1}{2} d^\top H d$ gives the Newton step $d = -H^{-1} \nabla f$, when $H$ is invertible and positive definite. Here $H$ is singular. The equation $H d = -\nabla \ell = (-0.5, -1)$ has solutions, because the second row of $H$ is twice its first and the right-hand side obeys the same relation. But they are not unique: any $d$ with $0.25\,d_0 + 0.5\,d_1 = -0.5$ works (my reasoning).

## Numerical derivatives: check, don't trust

The code above uses central differences $\bigl(f(x + h e_i) - f(x - h e_i)\bigr) / (2h)$. Their truncation error is $O(h^2)$. A forward difference $\bigl(f(x + h) - f(x)\bigr)/h$ has error $O(h)$. Round-off adds noise that grows like $\varepsilon / h$, where $\varepsilon \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ is the machine epsilon of 64-bit floating point (float64). The two effects pull $h$ in opposite directions. The table below measures both for the derivative of $\log(1 + e^x)$ at $x = 1$, whose exact value is $\sigma(1) = 0.731059$.

![Accuracy in digits of forward and central differences against step size h from 1e-01 to 1e-14; central differences peak near h = 1e-5 and forward differences near h = 1e-8](diagrams/20.3-h-sweep.svg "Bar height is -log10 of the error. Taller is more accurate.")

```python
import numpy as np

sig = lambda z: 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-z))
phi = lambda t: np.logaddexp(0.0, t)                  # loss along the line w = t * (1, 0), starting at w = 0

# Taylor coefficients at t = 0: phi'(0) = sigma(0) = 0.5, phi''(0) = sigma(0)(1 - sigma(0)) = 0.25
c0, c1, c2 = np.log(2.0), 0.5, 0.25 / 2
first = lambda t: c0 + c1 * t
second = lambda t: c0 + c1 * t + c2 * t**2

for t in [1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"t = {t}: true = {phi(t):.6f}  first = {first(t):.6f}  second = {second(t):.6f}  error = {phi(t) - second(t):+.6f}")

# Derivative of softplus at x = 1 by finite differences (exact value sigma(1))
x = 1.0
exact = sig(x)
print(f"exact derivative = {exact:.6f}")
print("     h   forward error   central error")
for k in range(1, 15):
    h = 10.0 ** -k
    fwd = (phi(x + h) - phi(x)) / h
    cen = (phi(x + h) - phi(x - h)) / (2 * h)
    print(f"{h:8.0e}   {abs(fwd - exact):12.3e}   {abs(cen - exact):12.3e}")
```

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Transposes in the chain rule.** The Jacobian has shape outputs by inputs, and the chain rule uses $J^\top$. In the worked example $J$ is square, so using $J$ instead of $J^\top$ raises no shape error. It silently gives $J (1, 2)^\top = (10, 4)$ instead of the correct $(6, 6)$.

> [!TRAP]
> **Definiteness is a test at critical points.** A positive definite Hessian says a point is a local minimum only where $\nabla f = 0$. Elsewhere, curvature alone does not classify a point.

> [!TRAP]
> **Overflow in the softplus.** `np.log(1 + np.exp(z))` returns `inf` once $z$ exceeds about 709 in float64. Use `np.logaddexp(0.0, z)`, which computes the same value stably. The NumPy documentation describes `logaddexp` as $\log(\exp x_1 + \exp x_2)$ [2]; the overflow threshold is my reasoning.

> [!TRAP]
> **Step size.** A step $h$ that is too small gives round-off noise, and one that is too large gives truncation error. Check the step on a case where you know the answer, as the table above does.

## Summary

- The gradient collects the first partial derivatives. It points uphill and is perpendicular to level curves.
- The Jacobian of $F: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is $m \times n$. The chain rule multiplies transposed Jacobians in reverse order.
- The Hessian's eigenvalue signs classify a critical point: all positive is a minimum, mixed is a saddle.
- The second-order Taylor model is $f(x) + \nabla f \cdot d + \tfrac{1}{2} d^\top H d$, with error of order $\|d\|^3$ in general.
- Central differences with $h = 10^{-5}$ gave about 11 correct digits in float64, against about 8 at best for forward differences.

### Question 1: Calculate

Let $f(x, y) = x^2 y + y$. Find $\nabla f$ at $(1, 2)$.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$\partial f / \partial x = 2xy = 4$ and $\partial f / \partial y = x^2 + 1 = 2$. So $\nabla f(1, 2) = (4, 2)$.

</details>

### Question 2: Predict

For the one-sample logistic loss with $a = (1, 2)$, what is its Hessian at $w = (2, -1)$? Answer without computing the matrix from scratch.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$z = a \cdot w = 2 - 2 = 0$, so $\sigma(z) = 0.5$ and $H = 0.25\, a a^\top$. This is the same matrix as at $w = 0$: $\begin{pmatrix} 0.25 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The Hessian depends on $w$ only through $z$.

</details>

### Question 3: Find the bug

```python
import numpy as np

def grad(f, x, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(x.size):
        e = np.zeros_like(x)
        e[i] = h
        g[i] = (f(x + e) + f(x - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g
```

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

The bug is `+` where `-` belongs. The sum $f(x + e) + f(x - e) \approx 2 f(x)$ divided by $2h$ gives about $f(x)/h$. For $f = w_0^2 + w_1^2$ at $(1, 2)$ this is about $5 / 10^{-5} = 5 \times 10^5$, not the gradient $(2, 4)$.

</details>

### Question 4: Explain

Why is the gradient perpendicular to the level curve through $x$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Along a level curve $f$ is constant. For a tangent step $d$, the first-order Taylor term gives $f(x + d) - f(x) \approx \nabla f \cdot d = 0$. Since $d$ can be any tangent direction, $\nabla f$ must be orthogonal to all of them, so it is perpendicular to the curve.

</details>

### Question 5: Interpret the numbers

At $t = 2$ the first-order error is $2.126928 - 1.693147 = 0.433781$, and the second-order error is $0.066219$. Why does adding the $0.125\,t^2$ term shrink the error by a factor of about 6.5?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

The quadratic term adds $0.125 \cdot 4 = 0.5$ at $t = 2$. That is most of the gap between the first-order model and the true value. The remaining gap comes from higher-order terms. Here the cubic term is zero, so the remainder is fourth order.

</details>
