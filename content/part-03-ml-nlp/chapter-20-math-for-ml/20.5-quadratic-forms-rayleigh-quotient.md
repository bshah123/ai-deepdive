In this lesson you will decide whether a symmetric matrix is positive definite, bound a Rayleigh quotient with eigenvalues, and see why the first principal component is the direction of greatest projected variance. You will also find and fix a bug in a common positive-definiteness check.

**You need:** eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors (Lesson 20.2), gradients and Hessians (Lesson 20.3), and matrix–vector products (Lesson 20.1).

## The problem: is a critical point a bottom, a top or a saddle?

Training a model means minimising a loss $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$. At a stationary point $\theta^*$, where $\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta^*) = 0$, the second-order Taylor expansion gives

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta^* + \Delta) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta^*) + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta^\top H \Delta,
$$

where $H$ is the Hessian, the matrix of second derivatives. Whether the stationary point is a minimum, a maximum or a saddle depends on the sign of $\Delta^\top H \Delta$ across all directions $\Delta$. This expression is a **quadratic form**: a scalar $x^\top A x$ built from a matrix $A$.

Take $f(x) = 5x_1^2 - 4x_1x_2 + 2x_2^2$. Expanding $x^\top A x$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ gives exactly this $f$, so the matrix holds the curvature. The question for the rest of the lesson is how to read the sign pattern from $A$.

## Symmetry comes for free

A quadratic form is a scalar, so it equals its own transpose: $x^\top A x = x^\top A^\top x$. Averaging the two expressions gives

$$
x^\top A x = x^\top \left(\frac{A + A^\top}{2}\right) x,
$$

and the skew-symmetric part $(A - A^\top)/2$ contributes exactly zero. So we may assume $A$ is symmetric.

![Matrix A equals its symmetric part plus its skew part; x^T A x is 35 for A and for its symmetric part](diagrams/20.5-symmetrize.svg "A non-symmetric matrix, its symmetric part and its skew part. For x = (3, 1), the skew part adds 0.")

Take $A_{\text{raw}} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ -4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $x = (3, 1)$. Directly,

$$
x^\top A_{\text{raw}} x = 3(5 \cdot 3 + 0 \cdot 1) + 1(-4 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 1) = 45 - 10 = 35.
$$

The symmetric part is $\begin{bmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, and the same calculation gives $3(15 - 2) + 1(-6 + 2) = 35$ again.

## Definiteness: read the signs from the eigenvalues

For a real symmetric $A$, the spectral theorem gives real eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $v_i$. Writing $x = \sum_i c_i v_i$ and using $v_i^\top v_j = \delta_{ij}$ gives

$$
x^\top A x = \sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2 .
$$

The sign of this sum, for every $x \neq 0$, is decided by the signs of the $\lambda_i$:

| Class | Condition on $x^\top A x$ for all $x \neq 0$ | Eigenvalues |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Positive definite | $> 0$ | all $> 0$ |
| Positive semidefinite | $\geq 0$ | all $\geq 0$, at least one $= 0$ |
| Negative definite | $< 0$ | all $< 0$ |
| Indefinite | takes both signs | mixed signs |

By hand, $\det(A - \lambda I) = (5 - \lambda)(2 - \lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 1)$, so the eigenvalues of $A$ are 6 and 1. Both are positive, so $A$ is positive definite. The code below classifies three matrices and prints their eigenvalues.

```python
import numpy as np

def definiteness(M, tol=1e-10):
    S = 0.5 * (M + M.T)                    # the quadratic form only sees the symmetric part
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(S)            # ascending order
    if np.all(lam > tol):   return "positive definite"
    if np.all(lam >= -tol): return "positive semidefinite"
    if np.all(lam < -tol):  return "negative definite"
    if np.all(lam <= tol):  return "negative semidefinite"
    return "indefinite"

cases = [("bowl", [[5, -2], [-2, 2]]), ("saddle", [[2, 0], [0, -3]]), ("B", [[1, 10], [0, 1]])]
for name, M in cases:
    M = np.array(M, dtype=float)
    print(name, np.linalg.eigvalsh(0.5 * (M + M.T)), definiteness(M))
```

```output
bowl [1. 6.] positive definite
saddle [-3.  2.] indefinite
B [-4.  6.] indefinite
```

The third matrix, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 10 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, is not symmetric, and its symmetric part has eigenvalues $-4$ and $6$. It is indefinite, which matters for the trap later in this lesson.

![Level sets of a positive definite form are nested ellipses; level sets of an indefinite form are hyperbolas](diagrams/20.5-level-sets.svg "Left: f = 5x1^2 - 4x1x2 + 2x2^2, eigenvalues 6 and 1. Right: 2x1^2 - 3x2^2, eigenvalues 2 and -3. Each curve joins points with the same value of f.")

A positive definite $H$ at a stationary point means the loss rises in every direction, so the point is a strict local minimum. This follows from the expansion above (reasoning from the Taylor form, not a full proof; the remainder term must be small). An indefinite $H$ gives a saddle, where some directions go up and others go down.

Read the right-hand panel of the figure with this in mind. For $f = 2x_1^2 - 3x_2^2$, moving along the $x_1$-axis increases $f$, while moving along the $x_2$-axis decreases it. Gradient descent started near the origin in the $x_2$ direction would move away from it, which is why a saddle is not a minimum even though its gradient is zero there.

## The Rayleigh quotient is a weighted average of eigenvalues

For symmetric $A$ and $x \neq 0$, the **Rayleigh quotient** is $R_A(x) = \dfrac{x^\top A x}{x^\top x}$. It is invariant to scaling, $R_A(tx) = R_A(x)$, so only the direction of $x$ matters.

**Claim (Rayleigh–Ritz).** Let $\lambda_1 \geq \dots \geq \lambda_n$ be the eigenvalues of symmetric $A$. For every $x \neq 0$,

$$
\lambda_n \leq R_A(x) \leq \lambda_1,
$$

with equality at the eigenvectors $v_n$ and $v_1$.

*Proof.* Write $x = \sum_i c_i v_i$ and set $w_i = c_i^2 / \sum_j c_j^2$. Then

$$
R_A(x) = \frac{\sum_i \lambda_i c_i^2}{\sum_i c_i^2} = \sum_i w_i \lambda_i, \qquad w_i \geq 0, \quad \sum_i w_i = 1.
$$

A weighted average of the $\lambda_i$ lies between the smallest and the largest. It equals $\lambda_1$ only when all weight sits on eigenvalues equal to $\lambda_1$. For a simple top eigenvalue, that means $x \parallel v_1$. $\square$

The same result comes from Lagrange multipliers. Maximising $x^\top A x$ subject to $x^\top x = 1$ gives $\nabla_x\big(x^\top A x - \lambda(x^\top x - 1)\big) = 2(A - \lambda I)x = 0$, so $Ax = \lambda x$. The maximum exists because the unit sphere is compact.

![Dots at the Rayleigh quotients of random unit vectors; all lie between 1 and 6](diagrams/20.5-rayleigh-range.svg "Rayleigh quotients of random unit vectors for A = [[5, -2], [-2, 2]]. The values fill the interval between the eigenvalues and never leave it.")

The figure uses 20,000 seeded random unit vectors. The smallest and largest sampled values are 1.000 and 6.000 to three decimals, and the sample mean is 3.500. The expected value is exact: for a unit vector drawn uniformly from the circle, $\mathbb{E}[c_i^2] = 1/2$ in two dimensions, so $\mathbb{E}[R_A(x)] = (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)/2 = \mathrm{tr}(A)/2 = 3.5$ (reasoning from the proof above).

Worked example: for $x = (3, 1)$, $R_A(x) = 35/10 = 3.5$, which lies inside $[1, 6]$.

This is the optimisation view. Minimising $R_A$ over nonzero $x$ returns $\lambda_n$, and maximising it returns $\lambda_1$; the optimisers are the eigenvectors. Any method that searches over directions for the largest $R_A$ is therefore searching for the top eigenvector. The bounds above tell you the answer the search should reach, which is a useful check on an optimiser.

## Principal component analysis: the top eigenvector maximises variance

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ hold $n$ samples as rows, centred so that every column has mean zero. The variance of the projection onto a unit vector $v$ is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(Xv) = \frac{1}{n}\|Xv\|_2^2 = v^\top C v, \qquad C = \frac{1}{n} X^\top X .
$$

This formula assumes centred data and the $1/n$ convention (NumPy's `np.var` uses $1/n$ by default). Lesson 20.2 uses $1/(n-1)$ instead; that multiplies every eigenvalue by $n/(n-1)$ and leaves the eigenvectors unchanged. $C$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite (Question 1 below). By Rayleigh–Ritz, the largest value of $v^\top C v$ over unit $v$ is $\lambda_{\max}(C)$, reached at the top eigenvector $v_1$. That is why principal component analysis (PCA) keeps $v_1$ as its first component.

```python
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [-2.0, -1.0],
              [1.0, 0.5],
              [-1.0, -0.5]])               # four centred points (mean is zero)
n = X.shape[0]
C = X.T @ X / n                            # sample covariance, 1/n convention
eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(C)
v1 = eigvecs[:, -1]                        # eigh sorts ascending, so the top one is last
print("C =", C.tolist())
print("eigenvalues:", np.round(eigvals, 6).tolist())
print("v1 =", np.round(v1, 6).tolist())
print("var(X v1) =", round(float(np.var(X @ v1)), 6), " lambda_max =", round(float(eigvals[-1]), 6))
for th in [0.0, np.pi / 4, np.pi / 2]:
    v = np.array([np.cos(th), np.sin(th)])
    print("theta =", round(th, 4), " var(Xv) =", round(float(np.var(X @ v)), 6))
```

```output
C = [[2.5, 1.25], [1.25, 0.625]]
eigenvalues: [0.0, 3.125]
v1 = [-0.894427, -0.447214]
var(X v1) = 3.125  lambda_max = 3.125
theta = 0.0  var(Xv) = 2.5
theta = 0.7854  var(Xv) = 2.8125
theta = 1.5708  var(Xv) = 0.625
```

The top eigenvector is $v_1$ up to sign, and the variance along it equals $\lambda_{\max} = 3.125$. The other directions give less, as the three sampled angles show.

![Variance of the projection Xv against the direction angle; the peak sits at the principal eigenvector and equals lambda_max](diagrams/20.5-pca-variance.svg "500 synthetic 2-D points (seed 7). The curve is sampled every 2 degrees; the exact peak, 3.514 at 0.614 rad, lies inside the sampled cell that reads 3.513 at 0.628 rad.")

Keeping the top $k$ eigenvectors keeps the $k$ directions with the largest variance. Extending the bound from one direction to a $k$-dimensional subspace is the Courant–Fischer theorem. I could not open a source for that theorem while preparing this lesson, so the lesson does not rely on it; treat the $k$-direction claim as unverified until you check a textbook or lecture notes.

## Trap: a positive-definiteness check that accepts non-symmetric input

A common check is to call `np.linalg.cholesky` and treat success as "positive definite". The NumPy documentation states that no check is made for symmetry, and that only the lower or upper triangle is used (reference 1). For $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 10 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, the lower triangle is the identity, so the factorisation succeeds. Yet $x = (1, -1)$ gives $x^\top B x = -8 < 0$. The Cholesky check does not see the 10.

![Cholesky reads only the lower triangle of B, returns the identity without error, but x^T B x is -8 for x = (1, -1)](diagrams/20.5-cholesky-trap.svg "The non-symmetric input B passes the buggy check. The fix is to test symmetry first.")

For a symmetric input, success of the Cholesky factorisation is equivalent to positive definiteness, because the algorithm breaks down on a non-positive pivot (reasoning from the algorithm; the NumPy page says the input must be Hermitian and positive definite). So the test must check symmetry before calling Cholesky:

```python
import numpy as np

def is_pd_buggy(A):
    try:
        np.linalg.cholesky(A)
        return True
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
        return False

def is_pd(A, tol=1e-10):
    A = np.asarray(A, dtype=float)
    if not np.allclose(A, A.T, atol=tol):  # a non-symmetric matrix is not positive definite in this sense
        return False
    try:
        np.linalg.cholesky(A)
        return True
    except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
        return False

B = np.array([[1.0, 10.0], [0.0, 1.0]])
print("cholesky(B) =", np.linalg.cholesky(B).tolist())
print("buggy check :", is_pd_buggy(B))
print("fixed check :", is_pd(B))
x = np.array([1.0, -1.0])
print("x^T B x at x = (1, -1):", x @ B @ x)
print("fixed check on bowl matrix:", is_pd(np.array([[5.0, -2.0], [-2.0, 2.0]])))
```

```output
cholesky(B) = [[1.0, 0.0], [0.0, 1.0]]
buggy check : True
fixed check : False
x^T B x at x = (1, -1): -8.0
fixed check on bowl matrix: True
```

In practice, a robust test combines the two steps above: check symmetry with `np.allclose(A, A.T)`, then use either the Cholesky factorisation or the smallest eigenvalue, `np.linalg.eigvalsh(A).min() > 0`. The eigenvalue route costs more but states the definition directly (reasoning from the spectral form above).

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Cholesky does not check symmetry.** `np.linalg.cholesky` and `np.linalg.eigh` read one triangle and do not verify that the matrix is symmetric (reference 1 and reference 2). Test `np.allclose(A, A.T)` yourself.

> [!TRAP]
> **`eigh` sorts ascending.** The largest eigenvalue is the last one, so use `eigvecs[:, -1]`. `np.linalg.eig` does not guarantee any order, so the two functions cannot be mixed without care.

> [!TRAP]
> **"All entries positive" does not mean positive definite.** The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues 3 and $-1$, so it is indefinite.

> [!TRAP]
> **The PCA variance formula needs centred data.** For uncentred $X$, $\frac{1}{n}X^\top X$ is the second-moment matrix, not the covariance, and its top eigenvector is in general not the direction of maximum variance.

> [!TRAP]
> **The Rayleigh bounds need a symmetric matrix.** The proof uses an orthonormal eigenbasis, which a real symmetric matrix has. A non-symmetric matrix can have complex eigenvalues, and then the bounds $\lambda_n \leq R_A(x) \leq \lambda_1$ do not apply.

## Summary

- Only the symmetric part of $A$ affects $x^\top A x$: $x^\top A x = x^\top \frac{A + A^\top}{2} x$.
- A symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive.
- Rayleigh–Ritz: $\lambda_{\min} \leq \frac{x^\top A x}{x^\top x} \leq \lambda_{\max}$, with equality at the eigenvectors.
- The first principal component maximises the projected variance $v^\top C v$ over unit vectors $v$.
- A positive-definiteness check via Cholesky must test symmetry first.

## Quiz time

### Question 1: Prove the covariance is positive semidefinite

Let $C = \frac{1}{n}X^\top X$ for any real $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$. Show that $v^\top C v \geq 0$ for every $v$.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

For any $v \in \mathbb{R}^d$,

$$
v^\top C v = \frac{1}{n}(Xv)^\top (Xv) = \frac{1}{n}\|Xv\|_2^2 \geq 0 .
$$

The last step holds because a squared norm is never negative. So $C$ is positive semidefinite. Centring is not needed for this step; it is needed only for the variance interpretation.

</details>

### Question 4: Compute a Rayleigh quotient

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $x = (1, 1)$, compute $R_A(x)$. Use the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 3 = 0$ to check that the value lies between the eigenvalues.

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

$Ax = (5, 2)$, so $x^\top A x = 7$ and $x^\top x = 2$. Hence $R_A(x) = 3.5$. The roots of $\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 3 = 0$ are $\lambda = (5 \pm \sqrt{13})/2 \approx 4.303$ and $0.697$. Since $0.697 \leq 3.5 \leq 4.303$, the bound holds, and $A$ is positive definite.
