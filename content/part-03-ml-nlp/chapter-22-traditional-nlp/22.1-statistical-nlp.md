In this lesson you will turn text into numbers with three classical tools: **n-grams**, which count which words follow which; **TF-IDF** (term frequency times inverse document frequency), which weights each word by how informative it is; and **cosine similarity**, which compares two documents by the angle between their vectors. You will work through a four-document corpus by hand, then reproduce every number in plain Python and check it against scikit-learn.

**You need:** Python lists, dictionaries and `collections.Counter` (Lesson 6.4), plus the idea of a logarithm.

## The problem: a computer compares numbers, not sentences

The usual first step in Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a **bag of words**. Pick a fixed **vocabulary** (every distinct word in the corpus) and count how often each word occurs in each **document** (any piece of text you treat as one unit). Each document becomes a vector with one entry per vocabulary word.

Two problems follow. Word order is lost. And frequent words such as "the" dominate the counts, even though they say little about topic. The rest of this lesson deals with both.

Our corpus has N = 4 documents and V = 13 distinct words:

- d1: the cat sat on the mat
- d2: the cat ate the fish
- d3: the stock fell as prices rose
- d4: the stock prices rose again

## N-grams: counting neighbours

An **n-gram** is a run of n consecutive tokens. Bigrams (n = 2) are the windows of two adjacent words.

![Five bigram windows slide across the six tokens of "the cat sat on the mat"](diagrams/22.1-ngrams.svg "Bigrams: every window of two adjacent tokens.")

A bigram model predicts the next word from the one before it. The **maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)** is a ratio of counts:

$$P(w_2 \mid w_1) = \frac{c(w_1 w_2)}{c(w_1)}$$

Here $c(\cdot)$ counts occurrences in the corpus. Count "the" only where it is not the last word of a document, which gives six occurrences. It is followed by "cat" twice (d1, d2), by "stock" twice (d3, d4), by "mat" once and by "fish" once. So $P(\text{cat} \mid \text{the}) = 2/6 \approx 0.333$.

Most possible bigrams never occur. With V = 13 there are V² = 169 possible bigrams, and only 15 occur. The MLE gives every unseen bigram probability zero, so one unseen pair makes an entire sentence impossible. **Add-one (Laplace) smoothing** adds one to every count and adds V to the denominator:

$$P_{\text{add-1}}(w_2 \mid w_1) = \frac{c(w_1 w_2) + 1}{c(w_1) + V}$$

This gives unseen bigrams a small nonzero probability. The cost is that it takes probability mass away from bigrams that were seen (Jurafsky and Martin, SLP3 draft, ch. 3).

## TF-IDF: weight words by how rare they are

Let $\mathrm{tf}(t, d)$ be the raw count of term $t$ in document $d$. Let $\mathrm{df}(t)$ be the **document frequency**, the number of documents that contain $t$. The **inverse document frequency** is

$$\mathrm{idf}(t) = \ln\frac{N}{\mathrm{df}(t)}, \qquad \mathrm{tfidf}(t, d) = \mathrm{tf}(t, d) \cdot \mathrm{idf}(t)$$

where $\ln$ is the natural logarithm. Spärck Jones (1972) argued that terms should be weighted by how rarely they occur across a collection, so that a match on a rare, specific term counts for more than a match on a frequent one. Her paper gives that argument; the logarithmic form above is the standard convention, not something she derived in that paper.

![Bar chart of idf: the, in all four documents, has idf 0; cat and stock, in two documents, have 0.693; mat, in one document, has 1.386](diagrams/22.1-idf-bars.svg "idf for four words in the corpus.")

Work out d1 by hand. "cat" occurs once in d1 and in 2 of 4 documents, so its weight is $1 \times \ln 2 = 0.693$. "sat", "on" and "mat" each occur once in one document, so each has weight $1 \times \ln 4 = 1.386$. "the" occurs twice in d1 but has $\mathrm{idf} = \ln(4/4) = 0$, so its weight is zero. The vector for d1 is therefore (cat 0.693, sat 1.386, on 1.386, mat 1.386, the 0).

## Cosine similarity: direction, not length

The **cosine similarity** of two vectors is

$$\cos(a, b) = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert}$$

where $\lVert a \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_i a_i^2}$ is the Euclidean length. For non-negative weights it lies between 0 (no shared terms) and 1 (identical direction).

Multiplying $a$ by $\alpha > 0$ multiplies the dot product by $\alpha$ and the norm of $a$ by $\alpha$, so the factor cancels and the cosine does not change. Put in words: cosine compares direction and ignores length. The diagram shows a two-term toy case. Vector B is vector A doubled. Their cosine is 1 while the Euclidean distance between them is 2.236.

![Two arrows from the origin in the same direction, A = (1, 2) and B = 2A = (2, 4), with a dashed line between their tips; the note gives cosine 1.000 and Euclidean distance 2.236](diagrams/22.1-scale.svg "Same direction, different length.")

For d1 and d2, only "cat" is shared, with the weight 0.693 in both. The dot product is $0.693 \times 0.693 = 0.480$. The lengths are $\lVert d_1 \rVert = \sqrt{0.693^2 + 3 \times 1.386^2} = 2.499$ and $\lVert d_2 \rVert = \sqrt{0.693^2 + 2 \times 1.386^2} = 2.079$. So $\cos(d_1, d_2) = 0.480 / (2.499 \times 2.079) = 0.092$.

## Run it

The program below builds every quantity above from the four documents. It uses only the standard library.

```python
import math
import re
from collections import Counter

docs = [
    "the cat sat on the mat",
    "the cat ate the fish",
    "the stock fell as prices rose",
    "the stock prices rose again",
]

def tokenize(text):
    return re.findall(r"[a-z]+", text.lower())

def ngrams(tokens, n):
    return [tuple(tokens[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(tokens) - n + 1)]

tokenized = [tokenize(d) for d in docs]
vocab = sorted({w for toks in tokenized for w in toks})
print("vocabulary size V =", len(vocab))

# Part 1: bigram counts and a bigram language model
bigram_counts = Counter(g for toks in tokenized for g in ngrams(toks, 2))
context_counts = Counter(w for toks in tokenized for w in toks[:-1])  # w1 followed by something

def p_mle(w1, w2):
    return bigram_counts[(w1, w2)] / context_counts[w1]

def p_add1(w1, w2):
    return (bigram_counts[(w1, w2)] + 1) / (context_counts[w1] + len(vocab))

def sentence_prob(sentence, p):
    prob = 1.0
    for w1, w2 in ngrams(tokenize(sentence), 2):
        prob *= p(w1, w2)
    return prob

print("P(cat | the) =", round(p_mle("the", "cat"), 4))
for s in ["the cat sat", "the cat rose"]:
    print(f"{s!r:16} MLE = {sentence_prob(s, p_mle):.4f}   add-one = {sentence_prob(s, p_add1):.4f}")
print("distinct bigrams seen:", len(bigram_counts), "of V^2 =", len(vocab) ** 2)

# Part 2: TF-IDF and cosine similarity
N = len(docs)
df = Counter(w for toks in tokenized for w in set(toks))
idf = {w: math.log(N / df[w]) for w in vocab}

for w in ["the", "cat", "mat", "stock"]:
    print(f"{w:6} df={df[w]} idf={idf[w]:.4f}")

def tfidf(tokens):
    tf = Counter(tokens)                      # raw term counts
    return {w: tf[w] * idf[w] for w in tf}

def cosine(a, b):
    dot = sum(v * b.get(w, 0.0) for w, v in a.items())
    norm_a = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in a.values()))
    norm_b = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in b.values()))
    return dot / (norm_a * norm_b)

vecs = [tfidf(toks) for toks in tokenized]
print("d1 tf-idf:", {w: round(v, 4) for w, v in vecs[0].items() if v > 0})
print("\ncosine matrix (tf-idf):")
for i in range(N):
    print(" ".join(f"{cosine(vecs[i], vecs[j]):.3f}" for j in range(N)))

query = tfidf(tokenize("cat fish"))
for i, v in enumerate(vecs):
    print(f"query 'cat fish' vs d{i + 1}: {cosine(query, v):.4f}")

doubled = tfidf(tokenize(docs[0] + " " + docs[0]))
keys = set(vecs[0]) | set(doubled)
dist = math.sqrt(sum((vecs[0].get(w, 0) - doubled.get(w, 0)) ** 2 for w in keys))
print(f"d1 vs d1 repeated: cosine = {cosine(vecs[0], doubled):.4f}, Euclidean distance = {dist:.4f}")

raw = [Counter(toks) for toks in tokenized]
print(f"raw-count cosine d1 vs d2 = {cosine(raw[0], raw[1]):.4f}")
```

The output matches the hand calculations: $P(\text{cat} \mid \text{the}) = 0.333$, the idf values 0, 0.693 and 1.386, and $\cos(d_1, d_2) = 0.092$. The raw-count cosine of d1 and d2 is 0.668. Most of that similarity comes from the shared "the", which tf-idf removes.

Ranking documents for a query uses the same machinery. Weight the query "cat fish" with the same idf, then sort documents by cosine. d2 contains both words, d1 contains only "cat", and d3 and d4 contain neither.

The cosine matrix above uses the same tf-idf weights. The pairs d3 and d4 score 0.342 because they share "stock", "prices" and "rose".

### Compare with scikit-learn

scikit-learn's `TfidfVectorizer` uses a different idf and also L2-normalizes each row, so its numbers differ from ours. It divides each row by its Euclidean (L2) length. Its idf is $\ln\frac{1+N}{1+\mathrm{df}} + 1$ (`smooth_idf=True`), which gives "the" a weight of 1 instead of 0. The source file `sklearn/feature_extraction/text.py` states this formula in its docstring. Repeating our calculation with that idf reproduces scikit-learn to floating-point precision.

<details><summary>Run locally: needs pip install scikit-learn</summary>

```python
# needs: pip install scikit-learn (this output is from scikit-learn 1.9.1)
import math
import re
from collections import Counter
import numpy as np
import sklearn
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

docs = [
    "the cat sat on the mat",
    "the cat ate the fish",
    "the stock fell as prices rose",
    "the stock prices rose again",
]
print("scikit-learn", sklearn.__version__)
vec = TfidfVectorizer()                  # raw counts, smooth idf, L2 rows
X = vec.fit_transform(docs)
names = list(vec.get_feature_names_out())
for w in ["the", "cat", "mat", "stock"]:
    print(f"{w:6} sklearn idf = {vec.idf_[names.index(w)]:.4f}")
S = (X @ X.T).toarray()                  # rows are unit length, so dot = cosine
print("sklearn cosine matrix:")
for row in S:
    print(" ".join(f"{v:.3f}" for v in row))

# Our implementation, with sklearn's idf: ln((1 + N) / (1 + df)) + 1
N = len(docs)
toks = [re.findall(r"[a-z]+", d.lower()) for d in docs]
df = Counter(w for t in toks for w in set(t))
idf = {w: math.log((1 + N) / (1 + df[w])) + 1 for w in df}

def vector(tokens):
    tf = Counter(tokens)
    vec = {w: tf[w] * idf[w] for w in tf}
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(v * v for v in vec.values()))
    return {w: v / norm for w, v in vec.items()}

V = [vector(t) for t in toks]
M = np.array([[sum(a[w] * b.get(w, 0.0) for w in a) for b in V] for a in V])
print("max difference from sklearn:", f"{np.abs(M - S).max():.1e}")
```

Under scikit-learn's convention, d1 and d2 score 0.409, not 0.092, because the shared "the" keeps weight 1. Numbers from different libraries are comparable only within one convention.

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> A single unseen bigram gives the MLE a probability of zero, and that zero multiplies through the whole sentence. "the cat rose" scores 0.0000 under MLE and 0.0105 with add-one. Smoothing is not optional for sparse counts.

> [!TRAP]
> The textbook idf $\ln(N/\mathrm{df})$ gives "the" weight 0, while scikit-learn gives it weight 1. Both are valid conventions, but the same word gets different numbers. Check which one a library uses before comparing scores.

> [!TRAP]
> Raw counts let frequent words dominate. Raw-count cosine for d1 and d2 is 0.668, against 0.092 with tf-idf. Most of the raw similarity is "the".

> [!TRAP]
> Cosine divides by the norms. A query with no known words has norm zero, and the `cosine` function above raises `ZeroDivisionError`. Return zero, or reject the query, before calling it.

## Summary

- An n-gram model estimates $P(\text{next} \mid \text{previous})$ by counting. Unseen n-grams need smoothing, and add-one is the simplest form.
- TF-IDF multiplies a raw count by $\ln(N/\mathrm{df})$. A word in every document gets weight zero, and a word in one document gets the highest weight.
- Cosine similarity is the dot product divided by the product of the norms. It depends only on direction, so document length does not change it.
- Textbook idf and scikit-learn's smoothed idf give different numbers. Compare scores only within one convention.

## Quiz time

### Question 1: Calculation

"stock" occurs in d3 and d4. What is its idf under the textbook formula $\ln(N/\mathrm{df})$?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

$\mathrm{df} = 2$ and $N = 4$, so $\mathrm{idf} = \ln(4/2) = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$. The code prints `stock df=2 idf=0.6931`.

</details>

### Question 2: Prediction

Using the same corpus, what are the MLE and add-one estimates of $P(\text{again} \mid \text{rose})$? Bigram "rose again" occurs once, and "rose" occurs twice as a non-final word.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

MLE: $1/2 = 0.5$. Add-one: $(1 + 1)/(2 + 13) = 2/15 \approx 0.133$. Smoothing lowers the estimate for a bigram that was seen, because it moves probability to the 12 other possible continuations of "rose" that were unseen.

</details>

### Question 3: Bug-spotting

A colleague writes `idf = math.log(N / (df + 1))`. What goes wrong, and what should it be?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

For "the" ($N = 4$, $\mathrm{df} = 4$), this gives $\ln(4/5) \approx -0.223$. A negative idf means a word that appears in every document lowers the score of every document it appears in. The textbook formula is $\ln(N/\mathrm{df})$, which gives zero for "the". scikit-learn's $\ln((1+N)/(1+\mathrm{df})) + 1$ gives a positive value and is also valid, but it is a different convention.

</details>

### Question 4: Explanation

Why does a document repeated twice have cosine 1.0000 with the original, yet Euclidean distance 2.4992 from it? When would you not want this property?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Repeating the text doubles every tf-idf weight, so the vector is multiplied by 2. Cosine divides out the scale factor, so the angle is zero and the cosine is 1. The Euclidean distance is the length of the original vector, 2.4992, because the difference between the two vectors is the original vector itself. Cosine is wrong when length carries information, for example when a longer document is a stronger signal of relevance or popularity.

</details>
