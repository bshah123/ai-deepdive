Hybrid search runs a lexical retriever and a dense retriever over the same corpus, then merges two ranked lists into one. In this lesson you will merge two lists by hand with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) and with min-max normalization, and you will measure what each method throws away.

**You need:** the two score types from Lesson 36.1: BM25, a lexical score, and cosine similarity between embeddings, a dense score.

## The problem fusion solves

A lexical retriever scores each document with BM25. A dense retriever scores each document with the cosine similarity between its embedding and the query's embedding. Cosine similarity always lies in $[-1, 1]$. A BM25 score depends on term statistics and document length, so its range changes from one query to the next. If you add the two raw numbers, the retriever with the larger numbers decides the order.

![Two score lists for the same four documents: lexical bars run from 3.20 to 12.40, dense bars from 0.40 to 0.81, drawn on one scale](diagrams/36.2-scale-problem.svg "Same four documents, two scales. The lexical spread is about 22 times the dense spread.")

Take four documents, A to D. The lexical order is A, B, C, D. The dense order is C, A, D, B. Add the raw scores and you get A 13.19, B 9.50, C 8.51, D 3.72. The sum reproduces the lexical order exactly, so the dense list changes nothing.

## Approach 1: reciprocal rank fusion

RRF discards the scores and keeps only the ranks, counted from 1. A document's RRF score is

$$
\mathrm{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)}, \qquad k = 60,
$$

where the sum runs over the result lists $r$ that contain $d$. A document missing from a list adds nothing from that list. The original paper fixed $k = 60$ "during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation" (Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, 2009). Elasticsearch's RRF uses the same formula, with a default `rank_constant` of 60 (Elastic documentation).

Apply it to the example. A is first in the lexical list and second in the dense list, so its score is $1/61 + 1/62 = 0.03252$. C is third and first, so its score is $1/63 + 1/61 = 0.03227$. A wins by $1/62 - 1/63 \approx 0.00026$. The table shows every term.

![Table of ranks and per-list contributions 1 over (60 + rank) for A, C, B and D, with totals 0.03252, 0.03227, 0.03175 and 0.03150](diagrams/36.2-rrf-table.svg "RRF with k = 60. Each list adds one term, and the sum orders the documents.")

The constant $k$ sets how fast the contribution falls with rank. For rank 10 relative to rank 1, the ratio is $(k + 1)/(k + 10)$. That is 0.100 at $k = 0$, 0.55 at $k = 10$ and 0.87 at $k = 60$.

![Three pairs of bars: for k = 0, 10 and 60, the rank 10 bar relative to the rank 1 bar shrinks as k falls](diagrams/36.2-rrf-k.svg "Rank 10 relative to rank 1 for three values of k. Each rank 1 bar is full width.")

> [!KEY-INSIGHT]
> RRF depends only on ranks. Any strictly increasing transform of the scores, such as multiplying BM25 by 1,000 or taking its logarithm, leaves every rank and every RRF score unchanged. That immunity to units is why RRF is popular. The cost is that it discards how far apart the scores are. Bruch, Gai and Ingber (2023) state that "rrf ignores the raw scores and discards information about their distribution."

## Approach 2: normalize, then combine

Min-max normalization maps one list's scores onto $[0, 1]$ using the minimum and maximum of that list's candidates. A convex combination then weights the two normalized lists with $\alpha$:

$$
\tilde{s}(d) = \frac{s(d) - \min s}{\max s - \min s}, \qquad
\mathrm{score}(d) = \alpha\,\tilde{s}_{\mathrm{lex}}(d) + (1 - \alpha)\,\tilde{s}_{\mathrm{dense}}(d).
$$

The lexical scores run from 3.20 to 12.40, so A maps to 1.000, B to 0.641, C to 0.489 and D to 0.000. The dense scores run from 0.40 to 0.81, so C maps to 1.000, A to 0.951, D to 0.293 and B to 0.000. With $\alpha = 0.5$, fixed before looking at any results, the combined scores are A 0.9756, C 0.7446, B 0.3207 and D 0.1463. The order is A, C, B, D, the same as RRF.

![Two number lines: raw lexical scores from 3.20 to 12.40 at the top, and the same four documents after min-max at the bottom, from 0 to 1](diagrams/36.2-minmax.svg "The minimum always maps to 0 and the maximum to 1.")

Two properties follow from the formula. This is my own reasoning from the formula, not a cited result. First, min-max is unchanged by a positive scale factor or a shift applied to one list, because the numerator and the denominator change together. Second, the normalized values depend on the candidate set and on its most extreme score. One outlier sets the top of the range and squeezes every other document toward 0.

Bruch, Gai and Ingber (2023) compare convex combinations with RRF on in-domain and out-of-domain datasets. Their best convex variant uses a "theoretical" min-max, which takes the bounds of the scoring function rather than the candidate minimum and maximum. They report that "TM2C2 significantly outperforms rrf on all datasets in terms of NDCG." That variant tunes $\alpha$ on training data, and the paper also finds RRF "sensitive to its parameters."

## Run it: the hand example and a simulation

Real relevance judgments are not available here, so the script below builds a synthetic corpus with known relevance. The model is my own choice, not a benchmark. Each query has 1,000 documents and 5 relevant ones. The lexical score is about $10 \times (1.5 \cdot \mathrm{relevant} + \mathrm{noise})$ on a BM25-like scale, and the dense score is about $0.5 + 0.1 \times (1.2 \cdot \mathrm{relevant} + \mathrm{noise})$ on a cosine-like scale. Each method keeps the top 100 documents of each list, and a missing document counts as 0 in RRF and min-max. The raw sum uses the full corpus. Quality is measured with nDCG@10, normalized discounted cumulative gain over the top 10 results, which is 1 for a perfect list and lower when relevant documents sit lower. The script also multiplies the lexical scores by $c$, which changes the units without changing the order within the list.

```python
import numpy as np

# ---------- fusion methods -------------------------------------------------
def rrf(ranked_lists, k=60):
    """Reciprocal rank fusion: sum of 1/(k + rank) over every list a doc appears in."""
    total = {}
    for ranked in ranked_lists:                # each list: doc ids, best first
        for rank, doc in enumerate(ranked, start=1):
            total[doc] = total.get(doc, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return total

def min_max(scores):
    """Map {doc: score} to [0, 1] with this list's own min and max."""
    lo, hi = min(scores.values()), max(scores.values())
    if hi == lo:
        return {d: 1.0 for d in scores}
    return {d: (s - lo) / (hi - lo) for d, s in scores.items()}

def convex(lex, dense, alpha=0.5):
    """alpha * norm(lexical) + (1 - alpha) * norm(dense); a doc missing from a list counts as 0."""
    a, b = min_max(lex), min_max(dense)
    return {d: alpha * a.get(d, 0.0) + (1 - alpha) * b.get(d, 0.0) for d in sorted(set(a) | set(b))}

def ranking(scores):
    return sorted(scores, key=lambda d: (-scores[d], d))   # ties broken by id

def best_first(scores):
    return sorted(scores, key=lambda d: -scores[d])

# ---------- A. four-document hand example ----------------------------------
lex = {"A": 12.4, "B": 9.1, "C": 7.7, "D": 3.2}        # BM25-like scores
dense = {"A": 0.79, "B": 0.40, "C": 0.81, "D": 0.52}   # cosine-like scores
lex_order, dense_order = best_first(lex), best_first(dense)
print("lexical order:", lex_order, "| dense order:", dense_order)

raw = {d: lex[d] + dense[d] for d in lex}
print("raw sum      :", {d: round(v, 2) for d, v in raw.items()}, "->", ranking(raw))
rr = rrf([lex_order, dense_order], k=60)
print("RRF k=60     :", {d: round(v, 5) for d, v in rr.items()}, "->", ranking(rr))
cc = convex(lex, dense, alpha=0.5)
print("min-max 0.5  :", {d: round(v, 4) for d, v in cc.items()}, "->", ranking(cc))

# Scale test: multiply the lexical scores by c. Only the raw sum should react.
for c in (1.0, 0.05):
    scaled = {d: c * lex[d] for d in lex}
    r_raw = ranking({d: scaled[d] + dense[d] for d in lex})
    r_rrf = ranking(rrf([best_first(scaled), dense_order], k=60))
    r_mm = ranking(convex(scaled, dense, alpha=0.5))
    print(f"c={c:<5} raw:{r_raw} rrf:{r_rrf} minmax:{r_mm}")

# k sensitivity of one rank's contribution
for k in (0, 10, 60):
    print(f"k={k:<3} contribution of rank 1: {1/(k+1):.4f}  rank 5: {1/(k+5):.4f}  rank 10: {1/(k+10):.4f}")

# ---------- B. simulation with known relevance ------------------------------
# Assumed generative model (not a real benchmark): 1000 docs, 5 relevant per query.
# Lexical score ~ 10 * (1.5 * relevant + N(0,1)), shifted to be >= 0 (BM25-like scale).
# Dense score   ~ 0.5 + 0.1 * (1.2 * relevant + N(0,1)), cosine-like scale.
rng = np.random.default_rng(36)
N_DOCS, N_Q, N_REL, TOP = 1000, 300, 5, 100

def make_query():
    rel = np.zeros(N_DOCS, dtype=bool)
    rel[rng.choice(N_DOCS, N_REL, replace=False)] = True
    lex_s = 10.0 * (1.5 * rel + rng.normal(size=N_DOCS))
    lex_s -= lex_s.min()
    dense_s = 0.5 + 0.1 * (1.2 * rel + rng.normal(size=N_DOCS))
    return rel, lex_s, dense_s

QUERIES = [make_query() for _ in range(N_Q)]

def ndcg10(ranked, relevant):
    dcg = sum(1.0 / np.log2(i + 2) for i, d in enumerate(ranked[:10]) if d in relevant)
    idcg = sum(1.0 / np.log2(i + 2) for i in range(min(len(relevant), 10)))
    return dcg / idcg

def evaluate(c):
    """c scales the lexical scores (a change of units, e.g. BM25 vs. a rescaled BM25)."""
    names = ["lexical only", "dense only", "raw sum", "min-max a=0.5", "RRF k=60"]
    out = {n: [] for n in names}
    top1_agree = 0
    for rel, lex_s, dense_s in QUERIES:
        relevant = set(np.flatnonzero(rel).tolist())
        L = c * lex_s
        t_lex = [int(i) for i in np.argsort(-L, kind="stable")[:TOP]]
        t_den = [int(i) for i in np.argsort(-dense_s, kind="stable")[:TOP]]
        lex_top = {i: float(L[i]) for i in t_lex}
        den_top = {i: float(dense_s[i]) for i in t_den}
        raw_all = {i: float(L[i] + dense_s[i]) for i in range(N_DOCS)}  # raw sum over the full corpus
        mm = convex(lex_top, den_top, alpha=0.5)
        rr_s = rrf([t_lex, t_den], k=60)
        out["lexical only"].append(ndcg10(t_lex, relevant))
        out["dense only"].append(ndcg10(t_den, relevant))
        out["raw sum"].append(ndcg10(ranking(raw_all), relevant))
        out["min-max a=0.5"].append(ndcg10(ranking(mm), relevant))
        out["RRF k=60"].append(ndcg10(ranking(rr_s), relevant))
        top1_agree += ranking(mm)[0] == ranking(rr_s)[0]
    return {n: np.array(v) for n, v in out.items()}, top1_agree / N_Q

base, agree = evaluate(1.0)
print(f"\nmean nDCG@10 over {N_Q} queries (standard error in brackets), lexical scale c=1")
for n, v in base.items():
    print(f"  {n:<15} {v.mean():.3f} (se {v.std(ddof=1)/np.sqrt(N_Q):.3f})")
diff = base["RRF k=60"] - base["min-max a=0.5"]
print(f"  RRF minus min-max: {diff.mean():+.3f} (se {diff.std(ddof=1)/np.sqrt(N_Q):.3f})")
print(f"  top-1 agreement RRF vs min-max: {agree:.2f}")

scale_runs = {c: evaluate(c)[0] for c in (0.1, 1.0, 10.0)}
for c, res in scale_runs.items():
    print(f"  c={c:<5} raw sum {res['raw sum'].mean():.3f}   RRF {res['RRF k=60'].mean():.3f}   min-max {res['min-max a=0.5'].mean():.3f}")
same = all(np.allclose(scale_runs[c]["RRF k=60"], base["RRF k=60"]) and
           np.allclose(scale_runs[c]["min-max a=0.5"], base["min-max a=0.5"]) for c in scale_runs)
print("  RRF and min-max identical across scales:", same)
```

Three things stand out.

- Both fusion methods beat either retriever alone: 0.261 and 0.276, against 0.185 for lexical and 0.124 for dense.
- RRF beats min-max by 0.015, with a standard error of 0.008. That is under two standard errors, so this model does not show that RRF is better. The two methods agree on the top document for only 54% of queries.
- The raw sum depends on units. It scores 0.214 at $c = 0.1$, 0.188 at $c = 1$ and 0.185 at $c = 10$. At $c = 10$ the lexical score dominates and the raw sum is effectively lexical-only. RRF and min-max do not move at all, as the script's invariance check confirms.

These numbers hold only for this model. The signal strengths, the noise and the five relevant documents are my choices, and a different model can reorder the methods. No parameter was tuned on the test queries, and $\alpha = 0.5$ was fixed in advance. Real comparisons need real judgments, such as the Bruch, Gai and Ingber (2023) experiments cited above.

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> **Summing raw scores.** A BM25 score of 12 plus a cosine similarity of 0.8 is not a meaningful total. Normalize first, or use ranks.

> [!TRAP]
> **Min and max over dictionary keys.** `min(scores)` on a dictionary returns the smallest key, which is a document id, not the smallest score. Use `scores.values()`.

> [!TRAP]
> **Off-by-one ranks.** `enumerate(..., start=0)` gives the top document $1/k$ instead of $1/(k+1)$. That silently uses $k - 1$. The formula counts ranks from 1.

> [!TRAP]
> **Tuning on test queries.** Choosing $\alpha$ or $k$ by looking at test nDCG leaks the test set. Tune on a held-out validation set.

> [!TRAP]
> **Min-max on a flat list.** Scores 0.80, 0.79 and 0.78 map to 1.0, 0.5 and 0.0. A gap of 0.02 in cosine similarity looks as large as the full range. Min-max amplifies noise in nearly flat lists.

## Summary

- RRF sums $1/(k + \mathrm{rank})$ over the lists that contain a document. It uses only ranks, so it is immune to score units, but it ignores how far apart the scores are.
- The constant $k = 60$ comes from a pilot study (Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, 2009). A smaller $k$ lets the top rank dominate.
- Min-max maps each list onto $[0, 1]$ from its own candidates. A convex combination then weights the two lists by $\alpha$. It is invariant to scale but sensitive to outliers.
- A raw sum depends on each scorer's units, and a change of units can reorder the results.
- In a simulation with known relevance, both fusion methods beat either retriever alone. RRF and min-max differ by less than two standard errors.

### Question 2: Prediction

Multiply every lexical score in the hand example by 1,000 and recompute the orders. Which of the three methods (raw sum, RRF, min-max with $\alpha = 0.5$) can change its order?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Only the raw sum can change. With lexical scores multiplied by 1,000, the lexical term dominates, so the raw sum orders the documents A, B, C, D. RRF uses only ranks, and the lexical order is unchanged, so it stays A, C, B, D. Min-max is unchanged by a positive scale factor, so it also stays A, C, B, D. The script's scale test checks this at $c = 0.05$.

</details>

### Question 3: Bug-spotting

```python
def min_max(scores):
    lo, hi = min(scores), max(scores)
    return {d: (s - lo) / (hi - lo) for d, s in scores.items()}
```

What is wrong, and how do you fix it?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

`min` and `max` iterate over the dictionary's keys, which are document ids, not its values. So `lo` and `hi` are ids. The first subtraction `s - lo` then raises a `TypeError`, because it subtracts a string from a float. The fix is `lo, hi = min(scores.values()), max(scores.values())`. The version in the script also handles the flat case `hi == lo`, which would otherwise divide by zero.

</details>

### Question 4: Explanation

Under RRF with $k = 60$, a document ranked second in both lists scores $1/62 + 1/62 \approx 0.0323$. A document ranked first in only one list scores $1/61 \approx 0.0164$. Why does the first document win, even though the second has the lower best rank?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

RRF adds one term per list, so agreement across lists accumulates. A single first place is worth about one term, while two good places are worth two terms. This is a property of the formula, not of the data. It means RRF rewards documents that several retrievers agree on, which is often desirable in hybrid search.

</details>
