A prompt is read and billed token by token, so a shorter prompt costs less and starts sooner. In this lesson you will shrink one passage in two ways, token pruning in the style of LLMLingua and extractive sentence selection, implement both in NumPy, and measure which facts each one keeps.

**You need:** the idea of a token (Lesson 23.1) and conditional probability $p(\text{next} \mid \text{previous})$ at the level of a frequency table.

## The problem compression solves

Retrieval-augmented generation pastes retrieved passages into the prompt, and the model reads all of it. Every call pays for every input token, and a long prompt adds delay before the first output token. The LLMLingua paper reports a full-shot GSM8K prompt of 2,366 tokens (Jiang et al., 2023, Table 2 [1]). The question is which tokens the answer actually needs.

Two families answer it. Token pruning deletes single tokens; LLMLingua belongs to this family. Extractive selection keeps whole sentences and drops the rest. Figure 1 shows one passage handled both ways.

![Two panels compare token pruning, which keeps 9 of 18 scattered tokens and drops the name Lindqvist, with sentence selection, which keeps whole sentences 2, 4 and 6](diagrams/40.2-two-families.svg "Same passage, two compressions. The numbers come from the experiment below.")

## The idea: predictable tokens carry little information

Read "in the northern province of" and the next word is almost forced, so deleting it loses little. Read "Ada" and the next word could be almost anything. Information theory makes this precise. The surprisal of a token is $-\log_2 p(\text{token} \mid \text{context})$, measured in bits. A token with probability $1/2$ costs 1 bit; a token with probability $1/1024$ costs 10 bits.

LLMLingua scores each token by its conditional probability under a small language model. Its main configuration uses a 7-billion-parameter Alpaca model (Jiang et al., 2023, Sections 4.2 and 5.1 [1]). Ranking by $p$ and ranking by $-\log p$ give the same order, so the two views agree.

The assumption is heuristic: a predictable token is redundant. It fails when a predictable token is needed for grammar, or when the scorer cannot see why a fact matters to the question.

## Worked example, by hand

Our toy scorer predicts each word from the word before it. It mixes a bigram table (counts of word pairs) with a unigram table (word frequencies):

$$
p(w \mid v) = 0.5\; p_{\text{bigram}}(w \mid v) + 0.5\; p_{\text{unigram}}(w)
$$

Both tables use add-0.1 smoothing, so no probability is zero. The scorer is trained on about 185 words of unrelated prose, so it knows "the" well and has never seen "Lindqvist".

Take this sentence from the passage: "The bridge was designed by the engineer Ada Lindqvist, who had previously worked on harbour cranes." It has 18 tokens, counting punctuation as tokens. Its total surprisal is 130.5 bits, an average of 7.25 bits per token. The word "bridge" costs 9.40 bits, so $p = 2^{-9.40} \approx 0.0015$, about one chance in 676. The word "The" costs 3.20 bits, so $p \approx 0.11$.

![Horizontal bars show the surprisal of each of the 18 tokens; common words such as The and the are short, and unseen content words are long; hatched bars mark the tokens dropped at a 50% budget](diagrams/40.2-surprisal-bars.svg "Surprisal of each token. Solid bars survive a 50% budget; hatched bars are dropped.")

Notice that "Ada" and "Lindqvist" both cost 7.84 bits. The toy scorer has seen neither word, so it cannot tell a name from any other unfamiliar word. A larger scorer trained on more text would probably separate them; I have not tested that here. This limit comes back in the traps below.

## Code: a toy scorer and token pruning

The block below contains the whole mechanism. It uses only the standard library and NumPy, so it runs in the page's Python runner.

```python
import math
import re

import numpy as np

# Generic prose used only to train the toy language model. It contains no GOLD token.
BACKGROUND = (
    "The council met on Tuesday to review the budget for roads and water pipes. "
    "Most members agreed that the old pipes in the east district needed replacing before the next winter. "
    "The engineers explained that the work would take about two years and would close several streets "
    "for short periods. Residents asked how the costs would be shared between the city and the regional "
    "government. The mayor said that a full report would be published before the final vote. "
    "Later in the meeting, the committee discussed a plan for new bus lanes along the main road. "
    "Several drivers said that the lanes would reduce delays during the morning rush. "
    "Others worried that the lanes would take space from parking near the shops. "
    "The committee asked the transport office to measure traffic at the busiest junctions and report back "
    "in the spring. Meanwhile the library announced longer opening hours on weekends, and a new reading "
    "room for children will open in the autumn. The school board also reviewed plans for a sports hall "
    "that the local club had asked to share with students in the afternoons."
)


def tokenize(text):
    """Words and punctuation marks, one token each."""
    return re.findall(r"\w+|[^\w\s]", text)


def sentences(text):
    return [s.strip() for s in re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text.strip()) if s.strip()]


class BigramLM:
    """A toy causal language model: p(next word | previous word), interpolated with a unigram."""

    def __init__(self, corpus, k=0.1, lam=0.5):
        words = [t.lower() for t in tokenize(corpus)]
        self.vocab = sorted(set(words)) + ["<unk>", "<s>"]
        self.ix = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(self.vocab)}
        V = len(self.vocab)
        bigram = np.full((V, V), k)                  # add-k smoothed counts of (previous, current)
        unigram = np.full(V, k)                      # add-k smoothed counts of current word
        prev = self.ix["<s>"]
        for w in words:
            bigram[prev, self.ix[w]] += 1
            unigram[self.ix[w]] += 1
            prev = self.ix[w]
        bigram /= bigram.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
        unigram /= unigram.sum()
        # Unseen bigrams fall back to the unigram, so frequent words stay cheap and rare names stay costly.
        self.probs = lam * bigram + (1 - lam) * unigram[None, :]

    def surprisal(self, tokens):
        """Bits per token: -log2 p(token | previous token). High = surprising = informative."""
        out, prev = [], self.ix["<s>"]
        for t in tokens:
            cur = self.ix.get(t.lower(), self.ix["<unk>"])
            out.append(-math.log2(self.probs[prev, cur]))
            prev = cur
        return np.array(out)


def prune_tokens(tokens, lm, keep, seg=40):
    """Keep the top `keep` fraction of tokens by surprisal, segment by segment, in original order."""
    s = lm.surprisal(tokens)
    mask = np.zeros(len(tokens), dtype=bool)
    for start in range(0, len(tokens), seg):
        idx = np.arange(start, min(start + seg, len(tokens)))
        n_keep = max(1, round(keep * len(idx)))
        mask[idx[np.argsort(-s[idx], kind="stable")[:n_keep]]] = True
    return [t for t, m in zip(tokens, mask) if m]



lm = BigramLM(BACKGROUND)
sent = "The bridge was designed by the engineer Ada Lindqvist, who had previously worked on harbour cranes."
toks = tokenize(sent)
bits = lm.surprisal(toks)
for t, b in zip(toks, bits):
    print(f"{t:>12}  {b:5.2f} bits")
print("total bits:", round(float(bits.sum()), 2), "for", len(toks), "tokens")
kept = prune_tokens(toks, lm, keep=0.5)
print("kept at 50%:", " ".join(kept))
```

`prune_tokens` cuts the passage into segments of 40 tokens, keeps the highest-surprisal fraction of each segment, and restores the original order. Segments keep the scoring local. The real method conditions each score on the tokens already kept and works through the passage iteratively; the toy conditions only on the previous original token.

The output keeps 9 of 18 tokens: "bridge was designed by engineer Ada who previously harbour". Two things went wrong. "Lindqvist" was dropped, so the reader sees "engineer Ada" with no surname. "cranes" was dropped, so "harbour" hangs without its object. The result is no longer grammatical. Token deletion trades fluency for length, and a fact can disappear in the middle of a name.

What LLMLingua adds to this skeleton (Jiang et al., 2023 [1]):

- A budget controller that splits the token budget between the instruction, the demonstrations and the question.
- A coarse step that keeps or drops whole demonstrations before any token is touched.
- A token-level step that scores each token with the small model, conditioned on tokens already kept.
- On its tasks, the paper reports up to 20x compression "with little performance loss". On GSM8K, the quarter-shot setting keeps 117 tokens at 77.33 exact match, against 2,366 tokens at 78.85 for the full-shot baseline (Table 2 [1]). These are the paper's numbers on its datasets, not evidence about yours.
- The paper's threshold notation is hard to read in one direction. Check the released code before reimplementing it (Section 4.2 [1]).
- LLMLingua-2 replaces the language-model scorer with a token classifier on a bidirectional encoder, and reports 3x to 6x faster compression than LLMLingua. That is the authors' claim in the abstract; I have not checked it against the body (Pan et al., 2024 [2]).
- The official package is `llmlingua`. The README shows `PromptCompressor().compress_prompt(prompt, instruction=..., question=..., target_token=...)` [3]. I checked this against the README but did not run it, because it downloads model weights.

## Extractive selection: keep whole sentences

Sentence selection needs one score per sentence. The LSA-style method here builds a term-by-sentence count matrix $A$, with stopwords removed. It factorises $A$ by singular value decomposition, $A = U\Sigma V^\top$, and scores sentence $j$ by its length in the top $r = 3$ latent topics:

$$
\text{score}_j = \sum_{k=1}^{r} \left(\sigma_k\, v_{kj}\right)^2
$$

Here $\sigma_k$ is the $k$-th singular value and $v_{kj}$ is sentence $j$'s loading on topic $k$. A sentence that shares vocabulary with the strongest topics scores high. The code takes sentences in score order until the token budget is full, then prints them in their original order. This is my own implementation, not a library call, and it depends on $r$ and on the stopword list.

## Measuring it: which facts survive?

Each compressed output is scored on six key facts: "Tessel River", "1931", "Ada Lindqvist", "412 metres", "1962" and "twelve hangers". A fact counts as kept only if every one of its tokens survives. The passage is an invented 160-token text about a bridge, so no model can have memorised it.

![Line chart: key facts kept, out of 6, against the fraction of tokens kept, for four methods on one passage](diagrams/40.2-retention.svg "Measured on one passage. Each point is one budget; the lines join points of the same method.")

At a 50% budget, token pruning keeps 6 of 6 facts, first-k truncation keeps 4, random token deletion keeps 3, and sentence selection keeps 2. At 30%, the counts are 2, 2, 0 and 1. These are single-passage numbers, and each fact changes a count by one-sixth, so read the curves as a mechanism check rather than a ranking.

Why does pruning win here? Names and numbers are rare, so the toy scorer assigns them high surprisal, and the six key facts are exactly such tokens. The scorer and the metric are aligned by construction. A fair test has to measure answers from a model on a question set the scorer did not shape. Truncation does well because four of the six facts sit in the first half of this passage; move them and it falls behind.

The full experiment is below. It reproduces the sweep table and writes the data file behind the figures. The per-token bits in the worked example come from the earlier block, which runs the same functions.

    """Keep the top `keep` fraction of tokens by surprisal, segment by segment, in original order."""
    s = lm.surprisal(tokens)

    for start in range(0, len(tokens), seg):

        n_keep = max(1, round(keep * len(idx)))

def lsa_select(text, keep):
    """LSA-style extractive summary: score sentences by their length in the top-3 latent topics."""
    sents = sentences(text)
    terms = [re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", s.lower()) for s in sents]
    vocab = sorted({w for ts in terms for w in ts if w not in STOP})
    ix = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
    A = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(sents)))            # term x sentence count matrix
    for j, ts in enumerate(terms):
        for w in ts:
            if w in ix:
                A[ix[w], j] += 1
    _, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    r = min(3, len(S))
    score = ((S[:r, None] * Vt[:r]) ** 2).sum(axis=0)  # sentence weight in the top-r topics
    budget = keep * len(tokenize(text))
    chosen, used = [], 0
    for j in np.argsort(-score, kind="stable"):
        n = len(tokenize(sents[j]))
        if used + n <= budget:
            chosen.append(j)
            used += n
    return " ".join(sents[j] for j in sorted(chosen))

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> **A toy score is not the real score.** "Ada" and "Lindqvist" tie at 7.84 bits because the toy scorer has never seen either name. A larger scorer trained on more text will usually give such names different surprisals; I have not tested that here. Check which tokens your scorer keeps, not only the ratio.

> [!TRAP]
> **Rare is not the same as important.** Surprisal rewards names, numbers and typos alike. A corrupted number is also surprising, so the scorer keeps it.

> [!TRAP]
> **A token here is not a billed token.** In this lesson a token is a word or punctuation mark. Model tokenizers (Lesson 23.1) split text differently, so a 50% ratio here is not a 50% bill.

> [!TRAP]
> **Compression ratio is not answer quality.** At 15% token pruning keeps none of the six facts. Choose the budget by measuring answers on your own task.

## Summary

- Token pruning scores each token by surprisal, the bits it costs a language model, and deletes the predictable ones. This is the LLMLingua family.
- Sentence selection keeps whole sentences. Grammar survives, but weak sentences come along.
- At a 50% budget on one toy passage, token pruning kept 6 of 6 key facts and sentence selection kept 2 of 6. That is one passage, so treat it as a mechanism check.
- The score is only as good as the scorer: a bigram model cannot separate names it has never seen.
- Set the budget by measuring answers on your task, not by the compression ratio alone.

### Question 2: Prediction

Using the scorer from this lesson, which nine tokens does a 50% budget keep from the 18-token sentence? Explain the choice between the tied tokens "was", "designed", "by", "Ada", "Lindqvist", "worked" and "cranes".

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

The keepers are bridge, was, designed, by, engineer, Ada, who, previously and harbour. The top five scores (9.40, 9.40, 8.05, 8.05, 7.95) take bridge, engineer, who, harbour and previously. Four slots remain for the group that scores 7.84. The sort is stable, so ties go to the earliest positions: was, designed, by and Ada. Lindqvist, worked and cranes lose on position, not on content. A tie-break by position is an arbitrary rule and is part of why the name is lost.

### Question 3: Bug spotting

This function is meant to keep the most surprising tokens. Find the bug.

```python
import numpy as np

def prune_tokens_bug(tokens, lm, keep):
    s = lm.surprisal(tokens)
    n = round(keep * len(tokens))
    idx = np.argsort(s)[:n]        # keep the n lowest scores
    return [tokens[i] for i in sorted(idx)]
```

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

`np.argsort(s)` sorts ascending, so the slice keeps the least surprising tokens, which are the most predictable ones. That is the reverse of the goal. Use `np.argsort(-s)` to keep the highest scores. The function also ignores segmentation, so a long passage is scored as one block; that is a secondary issue.

</details>

### Question 4: Explanation

First-k truncation keeps 4 of 6 facts at a 50% budget, and sentence selection keeps 2. Why should you not conclude that truncation is the better method?

<details><summary>Show Solution</summary>

Truncation keeps the first half of this passage, and four of the six facts sit there, so the result reflects where the facts are placed. Truncation has no scoring step that can fail, but it cannot find facts that come later. One passage and six facts cannot rank methods. A fair comparison needs many documents, facts placed at random, and answers measured from a model.
