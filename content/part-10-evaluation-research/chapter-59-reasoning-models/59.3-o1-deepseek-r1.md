Reasoning models such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1 are large language models (LLMs) trained with reinforcement learning (RL) to write a long chain of thought (CoT) before they answer. In this lesson you will separate what these labs have published from what they have not, compute the group-relative advantage used by GRPO (group relative policy optimization) by hand, and measure how the choice of baseline changes the spread of a policy-gradient estimate.

**You need:** the CoT idea (Lesson 59.1) and the GRPO basics (Lesson 28.4).

## The problem

An LLM emits its answer one token at a time. On a multi-step maths problem, a single wrong step ruins the answer, and writing the steps first gives the model room to catch its own errors. Chain-of-thought prompting gets some of this for free. Training a model to produce useful steps on its own is harder. Supervised examples of good reasoning are expensive to write, so both labs asked whether RL with a checkable reward (is the final answer right?) could teach the behaviour directly.

## What OpenAI has disclosed about o1

OpenAI's September 2024 post says o1 is trained with a large-scale RL algorithm that "teaches the model how to think productively using its chain of thought." It also says the raw chain of thought is not shown to users; a model-written summary is shown instead. The post does not describe the architecture, parameter count, RL algorithm or reward model (OpenAI, 2024).

![Three columns: what users see (final answer and a summary of the chain of thought), what is hidden (the raw chain of thought), and what is not disclosed (architecture, parameter count, RL algorithm, reward model, reasoning-token count)](diagrams/59.3-o1-disclosure.svg "What the September 2024 post says, and what it leaves out.")

The reported benchmark numbers are for a specific setting. OpenAI reports that o1 averaged 74% (11.1 of 15) on the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination (AIME) with one sample per problem, against 12% for GPT-4o. Re-ranking 1,000 samples with a learned scoring function raised the figure to 93%. Those are single reported runs under "maximal test-time compute", and they are not a like-for-like comparison with a single sample.

> [!TRAP]
> A common claim is that o1 uses tree-of-thoughts, self-consistency or search. OpenAI's post does not say that. Whether o1 searches at inference time (Lesson 59.2) is undisclosed. Treat any architecture claim about o1 as speculation unless the lab publishes it.

## DeepSeek-R1: an open recipe

DeepSeek-R1 is the better-documented case. Its paper (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) describes two models. DeepSeek-R1-Zero starts from the DeepSeek-V3 base model with no supervised fine-tuning (SFT). It is trained with GRPO and two rule-based rewards: an accuracy reward (is the final answer correct?) and a format reward (is the reasoning inside `<think>` tags, and the answer inside `<answer>` tags?). The paper reports that AIME 2024 pass@1 (the fraction of problems solved with one sample) rose from 15.6% to 77.9% during training, and gives 79.8 for the final R1.

The full R1 adds supervised stages around the same RL step. The paper says the first of them is there to make the output readable and consistent in language.

![Five-stage pipeline: DeepSeek-V3 base, cold-start SFT, reasoning RL with GRPO, rejection sampling plus SFT, final RL; a dashed R1-Zero shortcut skips the two SFT stages](diagrams/59.3-r1-pipeline.svg "The R1 pipeline (solid) and the R1-Zero shortcut (dashed).")

The stages, in order, as the paper describes them: SFT on thousands of long CoT examples to start the RL model; RL on math, code and logic with rule-based rewards; sampling the RL model and keeping only correct, readable traces for a second SFT round, which also adds non-reasoning data; and a final RL stage that mixes rule rewards with a learned reward model for helpfulness and harmlessness (DeepSeek-AI, 2025).

## GRPO: the group is the baseline

Policy-gradient RL raises the probability of answers that did better than expected and lowers the rest. "Expected" needs a baseline. Proximal policy optimization (PPO) learns a value model to supply it. GRPO, introduced in DeepSeekMath, drops the value model. It samples a group of $G$ answers to the same prompt and uses the group's own rewards (Shao et al., 2024):

$$
\hat A_i = \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}{\mathrm{std}(r_1,\dots,r_G)}
$$

The objective adds a clipped probability-ratio term (as in PPO) and a Kullback–Leibler (KL) penalty to a reference model. The KL term is estimated with $x - \log x - 1$, where $x = \pi_{\text{ref}}/\pi_\theta$. The advantage is one number per answer, applied to all of its tokens. The objective averages each answer's per-token terms over its length.

![Four sampled answers with rewards 1, 0, 0, 1 feed a box giving mean 0.5 and std 0.5, which gives advantages +1, -1, -1, +1; a note says no critic network is used](diagrams/59.3-grpo-group.svg "The group supplies its own baseline.")

![PPO needs a learned value model in its advantage step; GRPO replaces that step with group statistics from G sampled answers](diagrams/59.3-ppo-vs-grpo.svg "Where the baseline comes from.")

### Worked example, by hand

Take the rewards $[1, 0, 0, 1]$. The mean is $0.5$. Each deviation is $\pm 0.5$, so the population standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.25} = 0.5$. The advantages are $\pm 1$: the two correct answers are pushed up and the two wrong ones down.

Take $[1, 1, 1, 0]$. The mean is $0.75$. The standard deviation is $\sqrt{(3 \cdot 0.25^2 + 0.75^2)/4} = \sqrt{0.1875} \approx 0.433$. The advantages are $0.25/0.433 \approx +0.577$ for each correct answer and $-0.75/0.433 \approx -1.732$ for the wrong one.

Check both groups in NumPy, along with a group where every answer is correct:

```python
import numpy as np

EPS = 1e-8

def grpo_advantages(rewards):
    r = np.asarray(rewards, dtype=float)
    return (r - r.mean()) / (r.std() + EPS)   # population std, as in the worked example

for group in ([1, 0, 0, 1], [1, 1, 1, 0], [1, 1, 1, 1]):
    print(group, "mean =", np.mean(group), "std =", round(float(np.std(group)), 3),
          "advantages =", np.round(grpo_advantages(group), 3))
```

```output
[1, 0, 0, 1] mean = 0.5 std = 0.5 advantages = [ 1. -1. -1.  1.]
[1, 1, 1, 0] mean = 0.75 std = 0.433 advantages = [ 0.577  0.577  0.577 -1.732]
[1, 1, 1, 1] mean = 1.0 std = 0.0 advantages = [0. 0. 0. 0.]
```

The all-correct group gets zero advantage everywhere, so it contributes no gradient. The epsilon keeps the division finite. The DeepSeekMath text does not say how equal-reward groups are handled; this is my reasoning from the formula.

## Rule-based rewards, and how they get gamed

A reward that is easy to check is easy to satisfy in the wrong way. The sketch below scores four completions to the question "3 × 4 = ?" with an accuracy term and a format term.

```python
import re

def reward(completion: str, gold: str) -> dict:
    """Rule-based reward: accuracy (1 if the answer matches) plus format (1 if the tags are present)."""
    m = re.search(r"<answer>(.*?)</answer>", completion, re.S)
    fmt = 1.0 if re.search(r"<think>.*?</think>\s*<answer>.*?</answer>", completion, re.S) else 0.0
    acc = 1.0 if m and m.group(1).strip() == gold else 0.0
    return {"format": fmt, "accuracy": acc, "total": acc + fmt}

samples = {
    "A: tags, correct":     "<think>3 * 4 = 12</think> <answer>12</answer>",
    "B: no think, correct": "<answer>12</answer>",
    "C: tags, wrong":       "<think>3 * 4 = 10</think> <answer>10</answer>",
    "D: no tags, wrong":    "The answer is 10.",
}
for name, text in samples.items():
    print(f"{name:22s}", reward(text, gold="12"))
```

```output
A: tags, correct       {'format': 1.0, 'accuracy': 1.0, 'total': 2.0}
B: no think, correct   {'format': 0.0, 'accuracy': 1.0, 'total': 1.0}
C: tags, wrong         {'format': 1.0, 'accuracy': 0.0, 'total': 1.0}
D: no tags, wrong      {'format': 0.0, 'accuracy': 0.0, 'total': 0.0}
```

A format-only grader would give answer C the same reward as answer A. An accuracy-only grader would give B the same reward as A. The two terms are needed together. The paper does not give the numeric scale of its format term in the text I read, so the 0/1 values above are my own toy choice.

## Does the baseline matter? A small experiment

Real GRPO training needs a model, so this experiment uses a toy. One prompt, and a policy that answers correctly with probability $q = \mathrm{sigmoid}(\theta)$. The reward is 1 for a correct answer and 0 otherwise. The exact gradient of the expected reward with respect to $\theta$ is $q(1-q)$. We compare three estimators built from $G = 8$ samples: no baseline, the group mean as baseline, and GRPO's mean-and-std advantage. The script draws 200,000 independent groups for each $q$.

```python
import numpy as np

G = 8              # completions per prompt (the GRPO group size)
TRIALS = 200_000   # independent gradient estimates per setting
EPS = 1e-8
rng = np.random.default_rng(59)

for q in (0.1, 0.5, 0.9):
    a = (rng.random((TRIALS, G)) < q).astype(np.int64)  # 1 = correct answer
    r = a.astype(float)
    score = a - q                                       # d/dtheta log pi(a) for q = sigmoid(theta)
    mean = r.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    std = r.std(axis=1, keepdims=True)                  # population std
    estimators = {
        "no baseline": r,
        "group mean": r - mean,
        "GRPO": (r - mean) / (std + EPS),
    }
    exact = q * (1 - q)
    print(f"q={q}: exact gradient = {exact:.3f}, all-equal groups = {np.mean(a.min(1) == a.max(1)):.3f}")
    for name, adv in estimators.items():
        g = (adv * score).mean(axis=1)                  # one gradient estimate per trial
        print(f"  {name:12s} mean = {g.mean():+.3f}  std = {g.std():.3f}")
```

```output
q=0.1: exact gradient = 0.090, all-equal groups = 0.430
  no baseline  mean = +0.090  std = 0.095
  group mean   mean = +0.079  std = 0.076
  GRPO         mean = +0.210  std = 0.187
q=0.5: exact gradient = 0.250, all-equal groups = 0.008
  no baseline  mean = +0.250  std = 0.088
  group mean   mean = +0.219  std = 0.041
  GRPO         mean = +0.464  std = 0.059
q=0.9: exact gradient = 0.090, all-equal groups = 0.430
  no baseline  mean = +0.090  std = 0.011
  group mean   mean = +0.079  std = 0.076
  GRPO         mean = +0.209  std = 0.187
```

![Bar chart of the standard deviation of one gradient estimate for three estimators at q = 0.1, 0.5 and 0.9; GRPO has the tallest bars at the extremes](diagrams/59.3-estimator-spread.svg "Spread of one 8-sample gradient estimate, from the experiment above.")

Three things stand out. The no-baseline estimator is unbiased (its mean matches 0.090 and 0.250). The group-mean estimator is biased by a factor of $(G-1)/G = 7/8$. This follows because the group mean includes the sample being scored, so $\mathbb{E}[(r_i - \bar r)\,s_i] = (1 - 1/G)\,\mathbb{E}[r_i s_i]$, and $0.875 \times 0.25 = 0.219$ matches the table. GRPO's mean is about 2.3 times the exact value at $q = 0.1$ and 0.9, and about 1.9 times at $q = 0.5$. Its scale depends on the success rate, because each group is divided by its own standard deviation.

> [!WARNING]
> This is a one-prompt Bernoulli toy. It has no token sequences, no clipping, no KL term and no length normalisation, and real GRPO averages over many prompts. The result shows the mechanism, not how R1 behaves. My reading that std normalisation shifts weight towards hard and easy prompts is reasoning from the formula, not something I measured on a language model.

## Common traps

> [!TRAP]
> Do not read architecture into the o1 post. The post describes training with RL on the chain of thought and says nothing about the model's structure.

> [!TRAP]
> A reward that checks only one part of the output will be gamed. Check the accuracy term and the format term separately, as in the four samples above.

> [!TRAP]
> A group in which every answer is correct, or every answer is wrong, gives zero advantage. At $q = 0.1$, 43% of groups of 8 are all-equal in the experiment, so most of the batch can contribute no gradient.

> [!TRAP]
> Do not compare pass@1 with majority-vote or re-ranked numbers. OpenAI's 93% uses 1,000 samples and a learned scorer; its 74% uses one sample.

> [!TRAP]
> Longer reasoning is not better reasoning. The R1 paper reports that response length grew during training; length alone does not show that the steps are correct.

## Summary

- OpenAI's o1 post discloses RL on chain of thought and hidden raw reasoning. It discloses no architecture, so ToT-style claims about o1 are unsupported.
- DeepSeek-R1-Zero applies GRPO directly to a base model with rule-based accuracy and format rewards. The full R1 adds SFT stages around the same RL.
- GRPO replaces PPO's learned value model with the mean and standard deviation of a group of sampled answers.
- In a one-prompt toy, the group mean is biased by $(G-1)/G$, and GRPO's std scaling changes both bias and spread, depending on the success rate.

## Quiz time

### Question 1: Compute the advantages

A group of four answers gets rewards $[0, 0, 1, 1]$ (two correct). Compute the GRPO advantages using the population standard deviation.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>
The mean is 0.5 and the standard deviation is 0.5. The advantages are $(0 - 0.5)/0.5 = -1$ for the two wrong answers and $+1$ for the two correct ones.
</details>

### Question 2: Predict the output

Using the NumPy snippet above, what is the advantage vector for the rewards $[1, 1, 1, 0]$ and is its sum zero?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>
The advantages are approximately $[0.577, 0.577, 0.577, -1.732]$. The sum is $3(0.577) - 1.732 \approx 0$. The advantages always sum to zero, since they are the rewards minus their mean, divided by a positive number.
</details>

### Question 3: Find the bug in an estimator

A colleague writes `adv = (r - r.mean()) / r.std()` for a batch of groups, then averages `adv * score` over all answers, and reports that the estimate matches $q(1-q)$. Name two problems.

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>
First, the division is not guarded against an all-equal group, where the std is 0. Second, the baseline is not the group's own mean, so it is not GRPO's baseline. Even with per-group means, the leave-in mean biases the estimate by $(G-1)/G$, and the std division changes the scale. So the claim that it matches $q(1-q)$ does not hold in general; the experiment above shows the gap.
</details>

### Question 4: Explain the disclosure

Why can't a reader conclude from OpenAI's o1 post that the model uses tree search at inference time?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>
The post does not describe the architecture or the inference procedure. It states that RL is used to train the chain of thought and that the raw chain is hidden. Search would need a separate disclosure, and none is in the post.
</details>
